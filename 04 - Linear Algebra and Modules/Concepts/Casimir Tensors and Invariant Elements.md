# Casimir Tensors and Invariant Elements

## Definition

Let $E$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $k$, and let $B:E\times E\to k$ be a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form. All tensor products below are over $k$.

> [!info] Casimir tensor
> Choose a basis $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ and its $B$-dual basis $v_1',\ldots,v_n'$, characterized by $B(v_i,v_j')=\delta_{ij}$. The **Casimir tensor** of $B$ is
> $$
> C_B=\sum_i v_i\otimes v_i'\in E\otimes E.
> $$
> It is independent of the chosen basis.

The **symmetric algebra** $S(E)$ is the quotient of $T(E)=\bigoplus_{r\ge0}E^{\otimes r}$ by the ideal generated by $x\otimes y-y\otimes x$. The image of $C_B$ in its degree-two part is the **Casimir polynomial**

$$
Q_B=\sum_i v_iv_i'\in S^2(E).
$$

For an associative $k$-algebra $D$ and a linear map $\mathcal D:E\to D$, multiplication gives the **Casimir element in $D$** associated with this data:

$$
\omega_{B,\mathcal D}=\sum_i\mathcal D(v_i)\mathcal D(v_i')\in D.
$$

Lang assumes that $\mathcal D$ is injective in Exercise 14(c). Basis independence of this expression holds for any linear $\mathcal D$.

## Why the Tensor Is Canonical

The form identifies $y\in E$ with the functional $B(y,-)\in E^\vee$. Hence it gives an isomorphism

$$
\Phi_B:E\otimes E\longrightarrow\operatorname{End}_k(E),
\qquad\Phi_B(x\otimes y)(z)=xB(y,z).
$$

To see the isomorphism explicitly, the usual tensors $e_i\otimes\varepsilon_j\in E\otimes E^\vee$ map to the matrix-unit operators in a basis $e_i$. The identification $E\simeq E^\vee$ then supplies the claimed map. Since $z=\sum_i v_iB(v_i',z)$, we have

$$
\Phi_B(C_B)=\operatorname{id}_E.
$$

Thus $C_B$ is the tensor that reconstructs each vector from its coordinates supplied by $B$. Changing the basis changes the coordinate description, while the identity operator stays the same.

## Key Properties

1. **Basis independence and functorial images.** The equality $C_B=\Phi_B^{-1}(\operatorname{id}_E)$ proves basis independence. Applying the fixed maps $x\otimes y\mapsto xy$ and $x\otimes y\mapsto\mathcal D(x)\mathcal D(y)$ proves the corresponding assertions for $Q_B$ and $\omega_{B,\mathcal D}$. The latter map preserves the order of the factors and does not require commutativity of $D$.
2. **Symmetry.** Because $B$ is symmetric, the basis dual to $(v_i')$ is $(v_i)$. Basis independence applied to $(v_i')$ therefore gives $C_B=\sum_i v_i'\otimes v_i$. The flip of the two tensor factors fixes $C_B$.
3. **Dependence on the form.** For $a\in k^\times$, the basis dual with respect to $aB$ is $(a^{-1}v_i')$. Hence $C_{aB}=a^{-1}C_B$, and the same scaling holds for its two images.
4. **Invariance under isometries.** If $T\in GL(E)$ preserves $B$, then $B(Tv_i,Tv_j')=\delta_{ij}$. Thus the transformed bases are dual and $(T\otimes T)C_B=C_B$.
5. **Equivariant algebra images.** Let a group $G$ act linearly on $E$ preserving $B$ and act on $D$ by $k$-algebra automorphisms. If $\mathcal D$ is $G$-equivariant, then $x\otimes y\mapsto\mathcal D(x)\mathcal D(y)$ is equivariant, so $\omega_{B,\mathcal D}$ is fixed by $G$. The induced action on $S(E)$ likewise fixes $Q_B$.

These arguments require neither characteristic zero nor division by $2$.

## Examples and Boundaries

> [!example] An orthogonal basis
> If $B(v_i,v_j)=b_i\delta_{ij}$ with every $b_i\ne0$, then $v_i'=b_i^{-1}v_i$, and
> $$
> C_B=\sum_i b_i^{-1}v_i\otimes v_i,
> \qquad Q_B=\sum_i b_i^{-1}v_i^2.
> $$
> This example assumes an orthogonal basis exists; the definition and proofs do not require one.

> [!example] Characteristic two
> Let $\operatorname{char}(k)=2$ and let $E=ke\oplus kf$ have $B(e,e)=B(f,f)=0$ and $B(e,f)=B(f,e)=1$. Its Gram matrix has determinant $1$, so $B$ is nondegenerate. The dual basis is $(f,e)$, giving
> $$
> C_B=e\otimes f+f\otimes e\ne0,
> \qquad Q_B=ef+fe=0.
> $$
> The two tensor summands are distinct basis elements, while multiplication in $S(E)$ identifies them. Thus a nonzero Casimir tensor can have zero Casimir polynomial.

> [!warning] Trace forms on trace-zero matrices
> For $E=\mathfrak{sl}_n(k)$ and $B(X,Y)=\operatorname{tr}(XY)$, testing off-diagonal units and diagonal differences proves
> $$
> \operatorname{rad}(B)=\{aI_n:na=0\}.
> $$
> Indeed, pairing to zero with $E_{ji}$ forces $x_{ij}=0$ for $i\ne j$, and pairing to zero with $E_{ii}-E_{jj}$ forces all diagonal entries to agree. Scalar matrices pair to zero with every trace-zero matrix. Consequently the present construction on $\mathfrak{sl}_n(k)$ requires $n\cdot1_k\ne0$. Lang XVI.15(d) omits this condition; the corresponding exercise note preserves and corrects the source statement explicitly.

> [!example] Basis independence does not imply centrality
> Take $k=\mathbb Q$, $E=ke$, $B(e,e)=1$, $D=\operatorname{Mat}_2(k)$, and $\mathcal D(e)=\operatorname{diag}(1,2)$. Then $\mathcal D$ is injective, but
> $$
> \omega_{B,\mathcal D}=\operatorname{diag}(1,4),
> \qquad\omega_{B,\mathcal D}E_{12}=E_{12},
> \qquad E_{12}\omega_{B,\mathcal D}=4E_{12}.
> $$
> Thus this Casimir element is not central in $D$. The central Casimir construction in a universal enveloping algebra uses additional Lie-algebra structure and invariance hypotheses.
