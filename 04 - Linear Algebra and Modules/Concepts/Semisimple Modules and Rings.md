# Semisimple Modules and Rings

## Definition

Rings and modules are unital unless explicitly stated otherwise. Modules are left modules.

> [!info] Simple and semisimple modules
> A module $L$ is **simple** if $L\ne0$ and its only submodules are $0$ and $L$.
>
> A module $E$ is **semisimple** if it is a direct sum of simple submodules. The sum may be infinite; the empty sum gives the semisimple zero module.
>
> Equivalently, $E$ is a sum of simple submodules, or every submodule $F\subseteq E$ has a submodule complement $F'$ with $E=F\oplus F'$. These equivalences are proved in Lang's §2.

> [!info] Semisimple ring
> Following Lang, a ring $R$ is **semisimple** if $1\ne0$ and $R$ is semisimple as a left module over itself. The extra condition $1\ne0$ excludes the zero ring, even though the zero module is semisimple.

> [!info] Semisimple operator
> For a finite-dimensional $k$-space $E$ and $A\in\operatorname{End}_k(E)$, Lang XVII.10 calls $A$ **semisimple** when $E$ is semisimple as a $k[t]$-module with $t$ acting as $A$, equivalently as a $k[A]$-module. This is a statement over the specified field $k$.

## Intuition

Semisimplicity means a module can be assembled from simple pieces with no obstruction to splitting submodules. The pieces need not be one-dimensional over the original field. For a ring, requiring its regular module to split forces every module over that ring to have the same property.

## Key Properties

1. **Submodules and quotients remain semisimple.** This is Lang XVII, Proposition 2.2. Arbitrary direct sums of semisimple modules are semisimple, by collecting their simple summands.
2. **Every module over a semisimple ring is semisimple.** A module is a quotient of a free module, and a free module is a direct sum of copies of $R$. Apply the previous property. This is the argument of Proposition 4.1.
3. **The regular decomposition is finite.** If $R=\bigoplus_{i\in I}L_i$, the element $1$ lies in the sum of finitely many $L_i$. Multiplication on the left then places every $r=r1$ in that same finite sum. Thus a semisimple ring is left Artinian.
4. **Artinian radical criterion.** For a nonzero left Artinian ring, $R$ is semisimple if and only if its Jacobson radical $J(R)$ is zero. The full independent proof is [[02 - Ring Theory/Exercises/Exercise R303 - Artinian Rings with Zero Radical Are Semisimple|Lang XVII.3]]. The Artinian hypothesis cannot be dropped.
5. **Commutative case.** A commutative semisimple ring is a finite nonempty product of fields. A nonzero finite-dimensional commutative $k$-algebra is semisimple if and only if it has no nonzero nilpotents. These conclusions are proved in [[02 - Ring Theory/Exercises/Exercise R305 - Commutative Semisimple Rings Are Products of Fields|XVII.6]] and [[02 - Ring Theory/Exercises/Exercise R306 - Reduced Finite Dimensional Algebras Are Semisimple|XVII.7]].

For property 3, the finite sum of simple modules has the descending chain condition. One way to check this without a dimension over a field is induction on the number of summands: in a descending chain, intersections with a fixed simple summand and images in the quotient eventually stabilize; equality of both intersection and image in two nested submodules forces equality of those submodules.

## Examples and Boundaries

- **Vector spaces:** Every vector space over a field is a direct sum of one-dimensional simple modules, by choosing a basis.
- **Matrix rings:** $\operatorname{Mat}_n(k)$ for $n\ge1$ is a direct sum of its $n$ column left ideals. Each is the natural simple module $k^n$: if a submodule contains a vector with a nonzero coordinate, matrix units and scalar multiples generate every coordinate vector. Hence the matrix ring is semisimple.
- **A nonsplit module:** Over $R=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$, the submodule $k\varepsilon$ of the regular module has no complement. A complement would be a line generated by $a+b\varepsilon$ with $a\ne0$, but multiplication by $\varepsilon$ gives $a\varepsilon\ne0$ in both summands. Thus $R$ is not semisimple.
- **Radical zero is insufficient without Artinianity:** $J(\mathbb Z)=\bigcap_{p\text{ prime}}p\mathbb Z=0$. For every nonzero integer there is a prime not dividing it. Nevertheless, $\mathbb Z$ has no nonzero simple submodule: every nonzero subgroup $d\mathbb Z$ contains $2d\mathbb Z$ properly.

### Semisimplicity versus diagonalization

Write the minimal polynomial of $A$ as a product of powers of distinct monic irreducible polynomials over $k$:

$$
m_A(t)=\prod_{i=1}^s p_i(t)^{e_i}.
$$

The criterion requested in Lang XVII.10 says that the operator is semisimple exactly when every $e_i=1$. Diagonalizability **over $k$** additionally requires all $p_i$ to have degree one. Diagonalizability over an algebraic closure additionally requires these irreducible factors to be separable. Over an imperfect field, an irreducible polynomial can have derivative zero and repeated roots over an algebraic closure; its corresponding simple module is still semisimple over $k$. The associated exercise note gives an independent complete proof and an explicit inseparable example.

For instance, the real matrix

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

has irreducible minimal polynomial $t^2+1$ over $\mathbb R$, so its real module is simple. It is not diagonalizable over $\mathbb R$ because it has no real eigenvalue.

- The original definitions, equivalence of the three module conditions, and Proposition 2.2 were checked in [S2, Ch. XVII, §2, printed pp. 645-646, PDF pp. 660-661]. Proposition 4.1 and the ring's $1\ne0$ convention were checked in [S2, Ch. XVII, §4, printed p. 651, PDF p. 666]. These are proved source results.
- The radical, commutative-ring, and operator criteria are assigned exercises, not proofs supplied by their problem statements. Their vault solutions are independent derivations. [S2, Ch. XVII, Exercises 3, 6-7, 10-11, printed pp. 661-662, PDF pp. 676-677]
- The finite regular decomposition and examples above are independent deductions. The general module equivalence uses the maximal-submodule and maximal-independent-family arguments based on Zorn's lemma, as in the source.
- A semisimple associative ring or module is different from a semisimple Lie algebra. The Jacobson radical of an associative ring is also different from the solvable radical of a Lie algebra.
