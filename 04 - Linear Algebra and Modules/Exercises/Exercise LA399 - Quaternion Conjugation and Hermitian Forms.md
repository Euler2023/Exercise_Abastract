# Exercise LA399: Quaternion Conjugation and Hermitian Forms

## Problem Statement

> [!question] Lang, Chapter XIII, Exercise 7
> Let $\mathbb H$ be the division ring over the reals generated by elements $i,j,k$ such that $i^2=j^2=k^2=-1$, and
> $$
> ij=-ji=k,\qquad jk=-kj=i,\qquad ki=-ik=j.
> $$
> Then $\mathbb H$ has an automorphism of order 2, given by
> $$
> a_0+a_1i+a_2j+a_3k\longmapsto a_0-a_1i-a_2j-a_3k.
> $$
> Denote this automorphism by $\alpha\mapsto\bar\alpha$. What is $\alpha\bar\alpha$? Show that the theory of hermitian forms can be carried out over $\mathbb H$, which is called the division ring of quaternions (or by abuse of language, the non-commutative field of quaternions).

> [!warning] Source issue: “automorphism” is false
> The printed word “automorphism” must be read as **anti-automorphism**: quaternion conjugation satisfies $\overline{\alpha\beta}=\bar\beta\,\bar\alpha$, not $\bar\alpha\,\bar\beta$. The source wording is retained above; the corrected law is used below.

## Hints

> [!hint]- Hint 1
> Write $\alpha=a_0+u$ with $u=a_1i+a_2j+a_3k$ and calculate $u^2$.

> [!hint]- Hint 2
> Check conjugation on the generators $i,j,k$ and their products, keeping their order.

> [!hint]- Hint 3
> On the right $\mathbb H$-module $\mathbb H^n$, try $h(v,w)=\sum_i\bar v_iw_i$ and define the conjugate transpose $A^*=(\bar a_{ji})$.

## Solution

> [!success]- Independent derivation, with the source correction
> Put $u=a_1i+a_2j+a_3k$. Each cross term in $u^2$ cancels with its reverse because distinct quaternion units anticommute. Hence
> $$
> u^2=-(a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2),\qquad
> \alpha\bar\alpha=(a_0+u)(a_0-u)
> =a_0^2+a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2.
> $$
> The same computation gives $\bar\alpha\alpha=\alpha\bar\alpha$. For $\alpha\ne0$ this number is a positive real scalar, so $\alpha^{-1}=\bar\alpha/(\alpha\bar\alpha)$. This also verifies directly the claimed division property.
>
> Conjugation fixes the real scalars and sends $i,j,k$ to their negatives. It is real-linear and involutive. The defining products give $\overline{ij}=\bar k=-k=(-j)(-i)=\bar j\,\bar i$, and similarly for $jk$ and $ki$; bilinearity gives
> $$
> \overline{\alpha\beta}=\bar\beta\,\bar\alpha,\qquad
> \overline{\bar\alpha}=\alpha.
> $$
> It cannot be a ring automorphism: $\bar i\,\bar j=k$ while $\overline{ij}=-k$.
>
> This involution supplies the usual hermitian algebra. Regard $\mathbb H^n$ as a **right** $\mathbb H$-module and set
> $$
> h(v,w)=\sum_{r=1}^n\bar v_r w_r.
> $$
> Then $h(v\alpha,w\beta)=\bar\alpha h(v,w)\beta$ and $h(w,v)=\overline{h(v,w)}$. Moreover,
> $$
> h(v,v)=\sum_{r=1}^n |v_r|^2\in\mathbb R_{\ge0},
> $$
> with equality only for $v=0$. Thus this is a positive-definite non-degenerate hermitian form.
>
> For a matrix $A=(a_{rs})$ acting by left multiplication on column vectors, put $A^*=(\bar a_{sr})$. Reversing order under conjugation gives
> $$
> h(Av,w)=h(v,A^*w),\qquad (AB)^*=B^*A^*,\qquad (A^*)^*=A.
> $$
> Consequently $(A^*)^{-1}=(A^{-1})^*$ for invertible $A$. A matrix $H=H^*$ defines a hermitian form $h_H(v,w)=v^*Hw$; it is non-degenerate exactly when $H$ is invertible. These definitions, adjoints, orthogonality, and the usual Gram-Schmidt procedure for positive-definite forms work over $\mathbb H$ when scalar order is retained.
