# Exercise LA459: Integral Alternating Forms and Symplectic Invariants

## Problem Statement

> [!question] Lang, Chapter XV, Exercise 17
> Let $E$ be a module over $\mathbb Z$. Assume that $E$ is free, of dimension $n\ge1$, and let $f$ be a bilinear alternating form on $E$. Show that there exists a basis $\{e_i\}$, $i=1,\ldots,n$, and an integer $r$ such that $2r\le n$,
>
> $$
> e_1\cdot e_2=a_1,\qquad
> e_3\cdot e_4=a_2,\quad\ldots,\quad
> e_{2r-1}\cdot e_{2r}=a_r,
> $$
>
> where $a_1,\ldots,a_r\in\mathbb Z$, $a_i\ne0$, and $a_i$ divides $a_{i+1}$ for $i=1,\ldots,r-1$, and finally $e_i\cdot e_j=0$ for all other pairs of indices $i\le j$. Show that the ideals $\mathbb Za_i$ are uniquely determined.
>
> **Printed hint:** Consider the injective homomorphism $\varphi_f:E\to E^\vee$ of $E$ into the dual space over $\mathbb Z$, viewing $\varphi_f(E)$ as a free submodule of $E^\vee$. Generalize to principal rings when you know the basis theorem for modules over these rings.
>
> **Following source remark:** “A basis as in Exercise 18 is called a symplectic basis.” The source then refers to Lang's *Introduction to Algebraic and Abelian Functions*, second edition, Chapter VI, §3, for an application to theta functions.

> [!warning] Source issues in the hint and cross-reference
> The map $\varphi_f(x)=f(x,-)$ has kernel equal to the radical of $f$ and need not be injective; the zero form is an immediate counterexample. The exercise allows degenerate forms, including $r=0$. Its image is still a free abelian subgroup of $E^\vee$. The following remark points to Exercise 18, although the displayed basis is the one in Exercise 17. We retain that printed reference and interpret it here as referring to the present exercise.

## Hints

> [!hint]- Hint 1
> Start with a nonzero pairing $a=f(e_1,e_2)$ in a basis. If another entry in the first or second row is not divisible by $a$, use a Bézout change of two basis vectors to replace $|a|$ by a strictly smaller positive integer.

> [!hint]- Hint 2
> Once $a$ divides both rows, make the remaining basis vectors orthogonal to $e_1,e_2$. If an entry in the remaining block is not divisible by $a$, add one of its basis vectors to $e_1$ and repeat the reduction. For uniqueness, inspect the ideals generated by minors of the matrix of $\varphi_f$.

## Solution

> [!success]- Independent construction, uniqueness proof, and generalization
> Write $x\cdot y=f(x,y)$. Alternation gives $x\cdot x=0$ and, by expanding $(x+y)\cdot(x+y)=0$, $x\cdot y=-y\cdot x$. In any basis the matrix $M$ therefore has zero diagonal and satisfies $M^{\mathsf T}=-M$. A basis change by $P\in GL_n(\mathbb Z)$ replaces it by $P^{\mathsf T}MP$.
>
> **1. A reducing basis operation.** If the form is zero, take $r=0$ and any basis. Otherwise some matrix entry is nonzero. Permute basis vectors and change a sign to arrange $a=e_1\cdot e_2>0$. Suppose $b=e_1\cdot e_j$ is not divisible by $a$, with $j\ge3$. Put $d=\gcd(a,b)>0$, write $a=da_0$, $b=db_0$, and choose integers $s,t$ with $sa_0+tb_0=1$. Replace
>
> $$
> e_2\longmapsto se_2+te_j,
> \qquad e_j\longmapsto-b_0e_2+a_0e_j.
> $$
>
> The determinant of this two-vector change is $sa_0+tb_0=1$, so it preserves the property of being a basis. The new first pairing is $sa+tb=d$, and $0<d<a$. If instead an entry $e_2\cdot e_j$ is not divisible by $a$, use the same operation with $e_2$ fixed and the pair $e_1,e_j$ varying; the pivot again decreases in absolute value.
>
> **2. Splitting the first alternating block.** After finitely many such strict decreases, the pivot divides all pairings in its first two rows, unless a later step produces a further decrease. At a stage where it does divide those rows, write
>
> $$
> e_1\cdot e_j=a q_j,\qquad
> e_2\cdot e_j=a p_j\qquad(j\ge3)
> $$
>
> and replace $e_j$ by $e'_j=e_j+p_je_1-q_je_2$. This is an invertible triangular basis change. Directly,
>
> $$
> e_1\cdot e'_j=aq_j-q_ja=0,
> \qquad e_2\cdot e'_j=ap_j-p_ja=0.
> $$
>
> Thus the matrix becomes $\operatorname{diag}(aJ,C)$, where
>
> $$
> J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}
> $$
>
> and $C$ is alternating on the remaining basis vectors. If some entry $c=e'_i\cdot e'_j$ of $C$ is not divisible by $a$, replace $e_1$ by $e_1+e'_i$. Its pairing with $e_2$ is still $a$, while its pairing with $e'_j$ is $c$. Step 1 now produces a strictly smaller positive pivot. Repeat the row checks and clearing. Every failure of divisibility strictly decreases the positive pivot, so this process terminates. At termination $a$ divides every entry of $C$ as well.
>
> **3. Induction and divisibility.** Apply the same procedure to $C$. The ideal generated by a matrix's entries is unchanged by an integral invertible basis change: new entries are integer linear combinations of old entries, and the inverse change gives the reverse inclusion. Since every entry of $C$ is divisible by the first pivot $a_1=a$, the next pivot $a_2$, if present, is divisible by $a_1$. Continuing gives
>
> $$
> P^{\mathsf T}MP
> =\operatorname{diag}(a_1J,\ldots,a_rJ,0_{n-2r}),
> \qquad 0<a_1\mid a_2\mid\cdots\mid a_r.
> $$
>
> Each step removes two basis vectors; the process stops at a zero block, so $2r\le n$. Reading the pairings in this basis proves existence, with positive generators chosen for the required ideals.
>
> **4. Uniqueness over $\mathbb Z$.** For $\ell\ge1$, let $\delta_\ell(M)$ be the nonnegative greatest common divisor of all $\ell\times\ell$ minors, and put $\delta_0=1$. These numbers are invariant under left and right unimodular transformations: the Cauchy-Binet formula expresses every new minor as an integer combination of old minors, and the inverse transformations give equality of the generated ideals. In particular they are invariant under congruence.
>
> Ordinary row or column permutations and sign changes turn each block $a_iJ$ into $\operatorname{diag}(a_i,a_i)$. Thus $M$ is equivalent, for calculating its determinantal divisors, to a diagonal matrix with nonzero entries
>
> $$
> d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_{2r}
> =a_1,a_1,a_2,a_2,\ldots,a_r,a_r,
> \qquad d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_{2r}.
> $$
>
> Every nonzero minor of a diagonal matrix is a product of selected diagonal entries. The divisibility chain shows that every $\ell$-fold such product is divisible by $d_1\cdots d_\ell$, and the first $\ell$ diagonal positions realize this product. Hence
>
> $$
> \delta_\ell=d_1\cdots d_\ell\quad(1\le\ell\le2r),
> \qquad\delta_\ell=0\quad(\ell>2r).
> $$
>
> The largest index with nonzero $\delta_\ell$ determines $2r$, and
>
> $$
> a_i=\frac{\delta_{2i-1}}{\delta_{2i-2}}\qquad(1\le i\le r).
> $$
>
> Thus the positive $a_i$, and therefore the ideals $\mathbb Za_i$ without any sign choice, are uniquely determined.
>
> **5. Extension to a principal ideal domain.** Let $R$ be a PID and $E$ finite free. Replace the integer gcd by a generator $d$ of $(a,b)$; the same Bézout operation is invertible. A failure of divisibility changes the pivot ideal from $(a)$ to the strictly larger ideal $(a,b)$. Such strict increases terminate because a PID satisfies the ascending chain condition: the union of an ascending chain is an ideal $(c)$, and its generator $c$ already belongs to one member of the chain. The block construction and divisibility chain therefore work over $R$. Determinantal ideals give uniqueness up to units by the same argument, using generators and cancellation in the domain. The zero block remains allowed.
>
> **6. Principal ideal rings with zero divisors.** For the more general commutative principal ideal ring, use the **Smith basis theorem over that ring**, as permitted by the final instruction, in two precise places. Its one-row case gives an invertible two-column change taking $(a,b)$ to $(d,0)$ with $(d)=(a,b)$, replacing the domain Bézout operation. Every ideal is principal, so the preceding ascending-chain argument still holds. When a pivot divides a row entry, choose any coefficient expressing that divisibility; the triangular clearing formulas require no cancellation. Thus the existence proof again gives alternating blocks with $a_i\mid a_{i+1}$.
>
> For uniqueness in this setting, do not divide determinantal divisors, since cancellation can fail. Each block $a_iJ$ is equivalent to two diagonal entries $a_i,a_i$. The uniqueness of the invariant ideals in the assumed Smith basis theorem therefore determines the sequence of paired ideals $(a_1),(a_1),\ldots,(a_r),(a_r)$ and the number of zero entries. Taking one ideal from each pair gives exactly the requested invariants.

## Notes

- **Source and proof status:** The statement and first part of the hint were checked on [S2, Ch. XV, Ex. 17, printed p. 598, PDF p. 613]; the rest of the hint, generalization, and following remark were checked on printed p. 599 / PDF p. 614. The integral and PID block constructions and uniqueness proofs are independently supplied, rather than invoking alternating normal form as its own proof.
- **Imported inputs:** The integer/PID proof uses Bézout's identity and the Cauchy-Binet formula, whose precise roles are stated above. The extension to principal ideal rings with zero divisors explicitly uses existence and uniqueness in the ordinary Smith basis theorem; that general theorem is not proved here.
- **Degeneracy and duality:** Over $\mathbb Z$, the displayed zero block spans $\ker\varphi_f$, so $\varphi_f$ is injective exactly when $2r=n$. Injectivity still need not mean that $\varphi_f$ is onto: a block $2J$ has injective adjoint map but index $4$ in its dual. A basis with all blocks equal to $J$ is available only when all invariant ideals are the unit ideal and there is no radical.
- **Terminology:** The source calls its integral normal basis “symplectic.” The coefficients $a_i$ record arithmetic information that cannot generally be removed by an integral basis change. “Dimension” of the free module means rank.
