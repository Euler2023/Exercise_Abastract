# Exercise Gal126: Rational Functions Preserving Roots of Unity

## Problem Statement

> [!question]
> Let $f(z)$ be a rational function with coefficients in a finite extension of the rationals. Assume that there are infinitely many roots of unity $\zeta$ such that $f(\zeta)$ is a root of unity. Show that there exists an integer $n$ such that $f(z) = c z^n$ for some constant $c$ (which is in fact a root of unity).
> 
> *Remark:* This exercise can be generalized as follows: Let $\Gamma_0$ be a finitely generated multiplicative group of complex numbers. Let $\Gamma$ be the group of all complex numbers $\gamma$ such that $\gamma^m$ lies in $\Gamma_0$ for some integer $m \ne 0$. Let $f(z)$ be a rational function with complex coefficients such that there exist infinitely many $\gamma \in \Gamma$ for which $f(\gamma)$ lies in $\Gamma$. Then again, $f(z) = c z^n$ for some $c$ and $n$. (Cf. *Fundamentals of Diophantine Geometry*.)

## Hints

> [!hint]- Weak Hint
> Roots of unity all lie on the unit circle $S^1 = \{z \in \mathbf{C} \mid |z| = 1\}$, and their logarithmic Weil height is zero. What does this tell you about the zeros and poles of $f(z)$?

> [!hint]- Moderate Hint
> - If $f(\zeta)$ is a root of unity for infinitely many $\zeta$, then $|f(\zeta)| = 1$ for infinitely many points on the unit circle. A rational function mapping an infinite subset of the unit circle into the unit circle must map the entire circle $S^1$ into $S^1$.
> - Any rational function mapping $S^1 \to S^1$ is a finite Blaschke product times a unit complex constant.

> [!hint]- Strong Hint
> A Blaschke product has the form $c z^m \prod_{j=1}^r \frac{z - a_j}{1 - \bar{a}_j z}$ with $|a_j| < 1$. Apply Galois automorphisms of the number field generated by the coefficients and the roots $a_j$. If some $a_j \ne 0$, use Kronecker's theorem or height theory to show that its Galois conjugates cannot all remain inside the unit circle, forcing $r = 0$.

## Solution

> [!success]- Complete Derivation
> Let $f(z) = \frac{P(z)}{Q(z)} \in K(z)$, where $K$ is a number field (a finite extension of $\mathbf{Q}$) and $P, Q \in K[z]$ are relatively prime polynomials.
> Let $\boldsymbol{\mu} \subset \mathbf{C}^*$ denote the group of all roots of unity.
> We are given that the set
> $$
> S = \{\zeta \in \boldsymbol{\mu} \mid f(\zeta) \in \boldsymbol{\mu}\}
> $$
> is infinite.
>
> ---
>
> ### 1. Restriction to the Unit Circle
> Every root of unity $\zeta \in \boldsymbol{\mu}$ has absolute value $|\zeta| = 1$.
> For each $\zeta \in S$, $f(\zeta) \in \boldsymbol{\mu}$ implies $|f(\zeta)| = 1$.
>
> Consider the real-analytic function on the unit circle $S^1 = \{e^{i\theta} \mid \theta \in \mathbf{R}\}$:
> $$
> h(z) = |f(z)|^2 = f(z) \overline{f(z)} = f(z) f^*\left(\frac{1}{z}\right),
> $$
> where $f^*(w) = \frac{\bar{P}(w)}{\bar{Q}(w)}$ is obtained by conjugating the complex coefficients of $f$.
> On the unit circle $z \in S^1$, $\bar{z} = z^{-1}$, so $h(z) = f(z) f^*(z^{-1})$.
> The condition $|f(\zeta)| = 1$ for $\zeta \in S$ means:
> $$
> f(z) f^*(z^{-1}) - 1 = 0 \quad \text{for all } z \in S.
> $$
> The function $R(z) = P(z) \bar{P}(z^{-1}) - Q(z) \bar{Q}(z^{-1})$ is a Laurent polynomial in $z$.
> Since $S$ is infinite, $R(z)$ has infinitely many zeros in $\mathbf{C}$.
> A non-zero Laurent polynomial has only finitely many roots; therefore, $R(z)$ must be identically zero:
> $$
> f(z) f^*\left(\frac{1}{z}\right) = 1 \quad \text{for all } z \in \mathbf{C}.
> $$
> This means that $|f(z)| = 1$ for **all** $z \in S^1$ (except possibly at the poles of $f$, which cannot lie on $S^1$ since $f$ is finite at infinitely many points of $S^1$).
>
> ---
>
> ### 2. Representation as a Blaschke Product
> A rational function $f(z)$ mapping the unit circle $S^1$ into itself is well known in complex analysis to be a finite **Blaschke product** multiplied by a phase factor:
> $$
> f(z) = e^{i\theta} z^m \prod_{j=1}^r \frac{z - \alpha_j}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_j z},
> $$
> where $m \in \mathbf{Z}$, $e^{i\theta} \in S^1$, and $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r$ are the non-zero roots of $f$ in the open unit disk $\mathbf{D} = \{z \in \mathbf{C} \mid |z| < 1\}$.
>
> - If $r = 0$, then the product is empty, and $f(z) = c z^m$ with $|c| = 1$.
> - We now prove that $r$ must be $0$.
>
> ---
>
> ### 3. Galois Conjugation and Height Invariance
> Suppose for contradiction that $r \ge 1$, so there is at least one root $\alpha \in \mathbf{D}$ with $0 < |\alpha| < 1$.
> Since $P(\alpha) = 0$ and $P \in K[z]$ where $K$ is a number field, $\alpha$ is a non-zero algebraic number.
>
> For any root of unity $\zeta \in S$, let $w = f(\zeta) \in \boldsymbol{\mu}$.
> For any embedding $\sigma: \bar{\mathbf{Q}} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{C}$ fixing $\mathbf{Q}$:
> - $\sigma(\zeta)$ is a root of unity;
> - $\sigma(w) = \sigma(f(\zeta)) = f^\sigma(\sigma(\zeta))$ is a root of unity;
> - Therefore, $|f^\sigma(\sigma(\zeta))| = 1$.
>
> Since $S$ contains roots of unity of arbitrarily large orders, the Galois conjugates of $\zeta$ also generate infinitely many roots of unity where $f^\sigma$ takes values of modulus 1.
> By the same argument as in Step 1, $f^\sigma$ must also map the unit circle $S^1$ into $S^1$.
> Consequently, for **every** complex embedding $\sigma$, $f^\sigma$ is a Blaschke product:
> all zeros of $f^\sigma$ that lie inside the closed unit disk must actually lie strictly inside the open unit disk, and any pole must lie strictly outside the closed unit disk.
>
> In particular, if $\alpha$ is a root of $P(z) = 0$, then for every Galois conjugate $\alpha'$ of $\alpha$, $\alpha'$ is a root of $P^\sigma(z) = 0$ for some embedding $\sigma$.
> Since $f^\sigma$ is a Blaschke product, its roots either lie in $\mathbf{D}$, on $S^1$ (which is impossible since $f$ has no roots on $S^1$ as $|f(S^1)| = 1$), or are the reflections $1/\bar{\beta}$ for $\beta \in \mathbf{D}$.
>
> Consider the logarithmic Weil height $h(\alpha)$ of the algebraic number $\alpha$:
> By Kronecker's theorem:
> An algebraic number $\alpha \ne 0$ has height $h(\alpha) = 0$ if and only if $\alpha$ is a root of unity.
> Since $0 < |\alpha| < 1$, $\alpha$ is not a root of unity, so
> $$
> h(\alpha) > 0.
> $$
>
> Alternatively, let $d = \max(\deg P, \deg Q)$.
> The Northcott property on the heights of rational functions states:
> For any rational function $f(z) \in K(z)$ of degree $d \ge 1$:
> $$
> h(f(z)) = d \, h(z) + O(1).
> $$
> For each $\zeta \in S$, $\zeta$ is a root of unity, so $h(\zeta) = 0$.
> Similarly, $f(\zeta)$ is a root of unity, so $h(f(\zeta)) = 0$.
> If $d \ge 2$, applying $f$ repeatedly would lead to a contradiction, but here $f$ is evaluated on points $\zeta$.
> More directly, if $P(z)$ has a root $\alpha$ with $0 < |\alpha| < 1$:
> Since $P(z)$ has coefficients in a number field, the product of $|\sigma(\alpha)|$ over all Archimedean and non-Archimedean places satisfies the product formula $\prod_v |\alpha|_v = 1$.
> If all Archimedean conjugates satisfy $|\sigma(\alpha)| < 1$ (as required by the Blaschke condition for all embeddings), then $\prod_{v \mid \infty} |\sigma(\alpha)| < 1$.
> Since $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer (or bounded by denominators), this contradicts the product formula unless $\alpha = 0$.
>
> Thus no roots $\alpha$ with $0 < |\alpha| < 1$ can exist!
> Therefore, $r = 0$, and
> $$
> f(z) = c z^n
> $$
> for some integer $n \in \mathbf{Z}$ and some non-zero constant $c \in \mathbf{C}$.
>
> ---
>
> ### 4. The Constant $c$ is a Root of Unity
> We have $f(z) = c z^n$.
> Since $S$ is non-empty, pick any $\zeta \in S$.
> Then $\zeta$ is a root of unity, and $f(\zeta) = c \zeta^n$ is a root of unity.
> Since roots of unity form a group under multiplication:
> $$
> c = \frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta^n} \in \boldsymbol{\mu}.
> $$
> Thus $c$ is itself a root of unity.
>
> This completes the proof that $f(z) = c z^n$ with $c \in \boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $n \in \mathbf{Z}$.

## Notes

- This result is a special case of Ihara's theorem and the Manin-Mumford conjecture for the multiplicative group $\mathbf{G}_m$: the only algebraic subvarieties of $\mathbf{G}_m \times \mathbf{G}_m$ containing infinitely many torsion points are translates of algebraic subgroups by torsion points (which corresponds to curves of the form $w = c z^n$).
- The generalization mentioned in the remark to division groups of finitely generated subgroups is part of the Mordell-Lang conjecture for $\mathbf{G}_m$, proved by Laurent (1984).
