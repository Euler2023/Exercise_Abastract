# Primitive Elements

## Definition

> [!info] Primitive element of a field extension
> Let $E/F$ be a field extension. An element $\theta\in E$ is a **primitive element for $E/F$** if
> $$
> E=F(\theta).
> $$
> Here $F(\theta)$ is the smallest subfield of $E$ containing $F$ and $\theta$. An extension generated by one element is called a **simple extension**.
>
> The base field is part of the definition: being primitive is a property of $\theta$ relative to $E/F$.

In general, elements of $F(\theta)$ are rational expressions $f(\theta)/g(\theta)$ with $f,g\in F[X]$ and $g(\theta)\ne0$. The definition itself does not require $\theta$ to be algebraic; the degree criteria and theorem below concern finite extensions.

## Intuition

An extension may initially be presented using several generators. A primitive element combines their information into one element from which all the original generators can be recovered. For instance, $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$ can be written as $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2+\sqrt3)$; the recovery formulas appear below.

For a finite extension of degree $n$, finding a primitive element turns field computations into polynomial computations modulo its minimal polynomial. It does not say that every nonzero element is a power of the primitive element.

## Key Properties

1. **Degree criterion.** If $[E:F]=n<\infty$ and $\theta\in E$, then
   $$
   E=F(\theta)
   \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
   \deg m_{\theta,F}=[F(\theta):F]=n,
   $$
   where $m_{\theta,F}$ is the minimal polynomial. Indeed, the minimal-polynomial degree formula and the tower law give $n=[E:F(\theta)]\deg m_{\theta,F}$.

2. **Power basis.** If $E=F(\theta)$ and $[E:F]=n$, then
   $$
   E\cong F[X]/(m_{\theta,F}),
   \qquad 1,\theta,\ldots,\theta^{n-1}
   \text{ form an }F\text{-basis}.
   $$
   Evaluation gives $F[\theta]\cong F[X]/(m_{\theta,F})$. This quotient is a field because the minimal polynomial is irreducible, so $F[\theta]=F(\theta)$. Polynomial division gives spanning, and a relation of degree less than $n$ would contradict minimality.

3. **Affine changes preserve generation.** For $a\in F^\times$ and $b\in F$,
   $$
   F(a\theta+b)=F(\theta),
   $$
   since $\theta=a^{-1}((a\theta+b)-b)$. In particular, primitive elements need not be unique.

4. **Proper intermediate fields detect failure.** An element $\theta\in E$ fails to generate $E/F$ exactly when it belongs to some proper intermediate field $F\subseteq M\subsetneq E$. One direction follows from $F(\theta)\subseteq M$; for the other, take $M=F(\theta)$.

5. **Exact algebraic criterion.** For an algebraic extension $E/F$, a primitive element exists if and only if there are finitely many intermediate fields. [[03 - Field Theory/Exercises/Exercise F123 - Finitely Many Intermediate Fields Imply a Primitive Element|Exercise F123]] proves that finiteness of the intermediate-field set implies simplicity, first proving finite degree rather than assuming it. [[03 - Field Theory/Exercises/Exercise F124 - A Simple Algebraic Extension Has Finitely Many Intermediate Fields|Exercise F124]] proves the converse by recovering each intermediate field from the coefficients of a minimal polynomial. Neither direction assumes separability.

6. **Integer-ring generation is stronger.** An integral primitive element in a number field need not generate its full ring of integers over $\mathbb Z$. Some number fields have no power integral basis for any choice of primitive element. See [[02 - Ring Theory/Concepts/Orders and Conductors|Orders and Conductors]] for the definitions and quotient obstruction.

## Primitive Element Theorem

> [!abstract] Finite separable extensions are simple
> If $E/F$ is **finite and separable**, there exists $\theta\in E$ such that $E=F(\theta)$.
>
> More precisely, if $F$ is infinite and $E=F(\alpha,\beta)$ is finite separable, then $\alpha+c\beta$ generates $E/F$ for all but finitely many $c\in F$.

This is the finite-separable consequence of Lang's Theorem 4.6. Artin's Theorem 15.8.1 states the characteristic-zero case, and the following page separately discusses finite base fields. Characteristic zero ensures separability; positive characteristic requires the stated hypothesis.

> [!success]- Proof: separating the embeddings
> **Finite base field.** If $F$ is finite, then finite-dimensional $E/F$ makes $E$ finite. Its multiplicative group $E^\times$ is cyclic. Choose a generator $\theta$; every nonzero element of $E$ is a power of $\theta$, and $0\in F(\theta)$, so $E=F(\theta)$.
>
> Cyclicity is an imported result, proved in Artin's Theorem 15.7.3(c), proof on printed p. 461 / PDF p. 473. The field-generation step is expanded here.
>
> **Infinite base field, two generators.** Suppose $E=F(\alpha,\beta)$ is finite separable of degree $n$. Use the embedding-count theorem: there are exactly $n$ distinct $F$-embeddings $\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_n:E\to\overline F$. For $i\ne j$, consider
> $$
> \sigma_i(\alpha)+c\sigma_i(\beta)
> =\sigma_j(\alpha)+c\sigma_j(\beta).
> $$
> If $\sigma_i(\beta)\ne\sigma_j(\beta)$, this equation has at most one solution $c\in F$. If those images of $\beta$ agree, the images of $\alpha$ differ, because the embeddings are distinct and $\alpha,\beta$ generate $E$; then it has no solution. There are only finitely many pairs, so only finitely many excluded values of $c$.
>
> Choose $c$ outside this finite set and put $\theta=\alpha+c\beta$. The $n$ elements $\sigma_i(\theta)$ are distinct roots of $m_{\theta,F}$. Hence
> $$
> n\le\deg m_{\theta,F}=[F(\theta):F]\le[E:F]=n.
> $$
> The degree criterion gives $E=F(\theta)$.
>
> **Several generators.** Any finite extension is generated as a field by a finite $F$-basis. Merge two generators at a time by the preceding argument. Every intermediate extension is finite separable, so the same hypotheses hold at each step. This proves the theorem.
>
> This expands Lang's source-contained embedding argument. Besides finite-field cyclicity, the imported inputs are the finite-separable embedding-count theorem, the minimal-polynomial degree formula, and the tower law; their general proofs are outside this note.

> [!warning] Scope of the theorem
> Finiteness and separability are sufficient conditions; separability is not necessary for an extension to be simple. Some purely inseparable finite extensions are simple, and others are not. The counterexample below shows why “every finite extension is simple” would be false.

## Finite-Field Terminology

> [!info] Two meanings of “primitive element”
> For a finite field $E$ of order $q$, **a primitive element of the finite field** commonly means an element $g\in E^\times$ of multiplicative order $q-1$:
> $$
> E^\times=\langle g\rangle.
> $$
> By contrast, **a primitive element of an extension $E/F$** means $E=F(\theta)$. The first condition implies the second for every subfield $F\subseteq E$, because all nonzero elements are powers of $g$. The converse need not hold.

Check the base field and the group mentioned in the problem. In particular, a generator of $\mathbb F_p^\times$ is also called a **primitive root modulo $p$**. Generating a field, generating its multiplicative group, and being a primitive root of unity are distinct conditions.

## Examples

> [!example] Recovering two square roots from one element
> Let $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$ and $\theta=\sqrt2+\sqrt3$. Since
> $$
> (\sqrt2+\sqrt3)(\sqrt3-\sqrt2)=1,
> $$
> we have $\theta^{-1}=\sqrt3-\sqrt2$, and therefore
> $$
> \sqrt2=\frac{\theta-\theta^{-1}}2,
> \qquad
> \sqrt3=\frac{\theta+\theta^{-1}}2.
> $$
> Both original generators lie in $\mathbb Q(\theta)$, so $E=\mathbb Q(\theta)$. In contrast, $\sqrt2$ alone does not generate $E$: if $\sqrt3=a+b\sqrt2$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Q$, squaring gives $ab=0$ and then either $a^2=3$ or $b^2=3/2$, neither of which is a rational square. See [[03 - Field Theory/Exercises/Exercise F16 - Primitive Elements of a Biquadratic Field|Exercise F16]] for the classification of all generators.

> [!example] A field generator that does not generate the multiplicative group
> Let $E=\mathbb F_3(i)$ with $i^2=-1=2$. The polynomial $X^2+1$ has no root in $\mathbb F_3$, so it is irreducible; thus $E$ has nine elements and $i$ generates $E/\mathbb F_3$.
>
> But $i^2=2\ne1$ and $i^4=1$, so $i$ has order $4$, whereas $E^\times$ has order $8$. It is primitive for the extension but not for the multiplicative group. By comparison, for $g=1+i$,
> $$
> g^2=2i,\qquad g^4=2,\qquad g^8=1.
> $$
> Thus $g$ has order $8$ and satisfies both conditions.

> [!example] A finite extension with no primitive element
> Let $t,u$ be algebraically independent over $\mathbb F_p$, and set
> $$
> E=\mathbb F_p(t,u),\qquad F=\mathbb F_p(t^p,u^p).
> $$
> [[03 - Field Theory/Exercises/Exercise F83 - Infinitely Many Intermediate Purely Inseparable Fields|Exercise F83(a)]] proves $[E:F]=p^2$ using the basis $t^iu^j$, $0\le i,j<p$.
>
> Every $\theta\in E$ satisfies $\theta^p\in F$: write it as a rational function in $t,u$ and apply Frobenius to its coefficients and variables. Its minimal polynomial therefore divides $X^p-\theta^p\in F[X]$, so $[F(\theta):F]\le p$. Since $p<p^2$, no element generates $E/F$. This is a purely inseparable extension.
