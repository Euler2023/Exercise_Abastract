# Exercise Rep147: The Representation Ring as a Special Lambda Ring

## Problem Statement

> [!question] Lang XVIII.25
> We now assume that you know §3 of Chapter XX.
>
> (a) Prove that the Grothendieck ring defined there for $\operatorname{Mod}_{\mathbb C}(G)$ is naturally isomorphic to the character ring $X(G)$.
>
> (b) Relate the above formulas with Theorem 3.12 of Chapter XX.
>
> (c) Read Fulton–Lang's *Riemann-Roch Algebra*, Chapter I, especially §6, and show that $X(G)$ is a $\lambda$-ring, with $\Psi^n$ as the Adams operations.
>
> *Original note:* For further connections with homology and the cohomology of groups, see Chapter XX, §3, and the references given at the end of Chapter XX, §3.

> [!info] Scope and source boundary
> $G$ is finite, and the representation category in (a) is the category of **finite-dimensional** complex $G$-modules; Lang makes this scope explicit in Chapter XX, printed p. 780, PDF p. 795. Here “$\lambda$-ring” includes the multiplication and iteration identities, often called a **special $\lambda$-ring**. Fulton–Lang's book was not found in the available local source collection, so its requested reading and proof are not being claimed as verified. The solution below independently proves the special identities, beyond the additive pre-$\lambda$ axioms.

## Hints

> [!hint]- Hint 1: Begin with the irreducible basis
> Maschke's theorem splits every exact sequence, so both the Grothendieck group and $X(G)$ have the irreducible representations as an integral basis. Exterior powers satisfy the addition formula, and the symmetric and exterior series are reciprocal after changing $t$ to $-t$.

> [!hint]- Hint 2: Split after restriction to cyclic subgroups
> Restriction to all cyclic subgroups is jointly injective on the character ring. On a cyclic group every representation is a sum of one-dimensional characters. Prove the multiplication and iteration identities there by symmetric polynomials, including virtual differences, and then use injectivity.

## Solution

> [!success]- Independent proof of the Grothendieck identification and special lambda structure
> **(a) The Grothendieck ring.** Let $R(G)=K_0(\operatorname{Rep}^{\mathrm{fd}}_{\mathbb C}(G))$. It is generated by isomorphism classes $[V]$, with relations $[V]=[U]+[W]$ for every exact sequence $0\to U\to V\to W\to0$. Multiplication is $[V][W]=[V\otimes W]$, with the diagonal $G$-action, and the unit is the trivial line.
>
> Maschke's theorem says that these exact sequences split. If $V_1,\ldots,V_s$ represent the irreducible modules, every module is a direct sum of these, with uniquely determined multiplicities. The functions taking an object to each multiplicity are additive on exact sequences. Consequently the $[V_i]$ freely generate $R(G)$ over $\mathbb Z$: they generate by decomposition, and these multiplicity functions prove their independence.
>
> Characters add under direct sums and multiply under tensor products, since $\operatorname{tr}(A\otimes B)=\operatorname{tr}(A)\operatorname{tr}(B)$. Thus
> $$
> \operatorname{ch}:R(G)\longrightarrow X(G),\qquad[V]\longmapsto\chi_V,
> $$
> is a unital ring homomorphism. It sends the free basis $[V_i]$ to the irreducible-character basis $\chi_i$, so it is a ring isomorphism.
>
> **(b) The symmetric–exterior identity.** Define $\lambda^r[V]=[\bigwedge^rV]$ and $\sigma^r[V]=[S^rV]$ for actual representations. Under $\operatorname{ch}$ these are exactly the operations denoted $\bigwedge^r(\chi)$ and $S^r(\chi)$ in Exercise 23. Evaluating their generating series at each $g\in G$ gives
> $$
> \sigma_t([V])(g)=\det(I-\rho(g)t)^{-1},\qquad
> \lambda_{-t}([V])(g)=\det(I-\rho(g)t).
> $$
> The character map is injective, so
> $$
> \sigma_t([V])\lambda_{-t}([V])=1
> $$
> holds in $R(G)[[t]]$. For $r=\dim V$, the coefficient of $t^n$, $n\ge1$, is
> $$
> \sum_{i=0}^r(-1)^i\lambda^i[V]\,\sigma^{n-i}[V]=0,
> \qquad\sigma^j[V]=0\text{ for }j<0.
> $$
> These are exactly the two identities of Lang XX, Theorem 3.12. Their formal logarithmic derivatives give the corrected formulas of XVIII.23(b), and coefficient comparison gives XVIII.23(c). Our proof establishes the representation-ring instance directly; it does not require the general Koszul-complex proof to which Lang refers.
>
> **(c1) Define operations on virtual classes.** The wedge basis decomposition gives a natural $G$-isomorphism
> $$
> \bigwedge^n(V\oplus W)
> \cong\bigoplus_{i+j=n}\bigwedge^iV\otimes\bigwedge^jW.
> $$
> Therefore $\lambda_t([V]+[W])=\lambda_t([V])\lambda_t([W])$. Since all series have constant term $1$, they are formally invertible. The universal property of the additive group completion extends this uniquely by
> $$
> \lambda_t([V]-[W])=\frac{\lambda_t([V])}{\lambda_t([W])}.
> $$
> This is independent of the chosen presentation of a virtual class. It gives $\lambda^0(x)=1$, $\lambda^1(x)=x$, $\lambda_t(x+y)=\lambda_t(x)\lambda_t(y)$, and $\lambda_t(1)=1+t$.
>
> **(c2) Specify the special identities.** We use the following universal polynomials with integer coefficients. For finite alphabets $X=(X_i)$ and $Y=(Y_j)$, let $e_r$ denote the elementary symmetric polynomial. The polynomial $P_n$ is characterized by
> $$
> e_n(\{X_iY_j\}_{i,j})
> =P_n(e_1(X),\ldots,e_n(X);e_1(Y),\ldots,e_n(Y)).
> $$
> The polynomial $Q_{m,n}$ is characterized by
> $$
> e_m(\{\textstyle\prod_{i\in I}X_i:|I|=n\})
> =Q_{m,n}(e_1(X),\ldots,e_{mn}(X)).
> $$
> The fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials, applied separately to each alphabet in the first formula, gives these integral polynomials. Weighted degrees bound the indicated indices; adjoining zero variables shows that the definitions stabilize once the alphabets are sufficiently large. The special identities to prove are
> $$
> \lambda^n(xy)=P_n(\lambda^1x,\ldots,\lambda^nx;
> \lambda^1y,\ldots,\lambda^ny),
> $$
> and
> $$
> \lambda^m(\lambda^n x)
> =Q_{m,n}(\lambda^1x,\ldots,\lambda^{mn}x)
> \qquad(m,n\ge1).
> $$
>
> **(c3) Prove them on a cyclic subgroup, also for virtual classes.** If $C\le G$ is cyclic, its complex irreducibles are one-dimensional: the operator for a generator is diagonalizable with roots of unity as eigenvalues, and its eigenspaces are invariant. Thus $R(C)=\mathbb Z[\widehat C]$, where multiplication of the basis elements is multiplication of the linear characters. For $x=\sum_{a\in\widehat C}n_a a$, the already defined operations satisfy
> $$
> \lambda_t(x)=\prod_{a\in\widehat C}(1+at)^{n_a},\qquad n_a\in\mathbb Z.
> $$
> If all coefficients in $x$ and $y$ are nonnegative, view them as lists of line characters with repetitions. The line characters of $xy$ are all products of a line in each list, so the definition of $P_n$ proves the product identity. The line characters of $\lambda^n x$ are all products of $n$ distinct positions in the list, so the definition of $Q_{m,n}$ proves the iteration identity.
>
> To include negative coefficients, fix $m,n$ and the finite character group $\widehat C$. In each fixed degree, the coefficient of the displayed product for $\lambda_t(x)$ is a polynomial over $\mathbb Q$ in the integers $n_a$, since the factors have coefficients $\binom{n_a}{j}a^j$. Hence, after expanding in the basis $\widehat C$, each coefficient of either proposed identity is a polynomial over $\mathbb Q$ in the finitely many coefficients of $x,y$. This is also true for iteration: first compute the polynomial coefficients of $\lambda^n x$, then substitute them into the polynomial formula for $\lambda^m$.
>
> These coefficient polynomials agree for all nonnegative integer choices by the preceding paragraph. A polynomial over $\mathbb Q$ vanishing on every tuple of nonnegative integers is zero, by the one-variable root bound applied successively to each variable. Thus the identities hold for all integer choices. Equality over $\mathbb Q[\widehat C]$ implies equality over $\mathbb Z[\widehat C]$, since this group ring is a free abelian group. This proves the special identities on all virtual classes of $R(C)$.
>
> **(c4) Descend the identities to $G$.** Restriction commutes with direct sums, tensor products, and exterior powers, and hence with $\lambda_t$ on virtual classes. The map
> $$
> R(G)\longrightarrow\prod_{C\le G\,\text{cyclic}}R(C)
> $$
> is injective: if a virtual character restricts to zero on every $C$, its value at each $g$ is zero by taking $C=\langle g\rangle$, and the character map in (a) is injective. The difference of either side of either special identity restricts to zero on every cyclic subgroup by (c3). Injectivity therefore proves both identities in $R(G)$. This establishes the special $\lambda$-ring assertion.
>
> **(c5) Identify the Adams operations.** Define $\psi^n$ by the formal identity
> $$
> -t\frac{d}{dt}\log\lambda_{-t}(x)
> =\sum_{n\ge1}\psi^n(x)t^n.
> $$
> The left side means $-t\lambda_{-t}'(x)/\lambda_{-t}(x)$; its coefficients are integral polynomials in the $\lambda^j(x)$, so lie in $R(G)$. On an actual representation, eigenvalues at $g$ show $\psi^n([V])(g)=\chi_V(g^n)$, by part (b) and the logarithmic-derivative calculation. Logarithmic differentiation is additive on products of series, so both sides are additive in virtual classes. Consequently $\psi^n=\Psi^n$ under $\operatorname{ch}$ on all of $R(G)$.
>
> In particular these are unital ring endomorphisms: for virtual characters $f,h$ and $u\in G$, one has $\Psi^n(fh)(u)=f(u^n)h(u^n)$, and additivity and preservation of $1$ follow just as directly. They also satisfy $\Psi^m\Psi^n=\Psi^{mn}$ and $\Psi^1=\mathrm{id}$, since $(u^m)^n=u^{mn}$. This identifies the Adams operations completely.

Adams operations need not preserve the effective cone or irreducibility for arbitrary $n$; see [[06 - Representation Theory/Exercises/Exercise Rep146 - Coprime Adams Operations Preserve Irreducibility|Lang XVIII.24]].
