# Exercise MF94: Theta Series and Sums of Two Six and Eight Squares

## Problem Statement

> [!question] Original Exercise 4.8.7
> (a) Recall $\theta(\tau,k)$ from Section1.2. Similarly to that section's proof that $\theta(\tau,4)\in M_2(\Gamma_0(4))$, show that $\theta(\tau,2)\in M_1(\Gamma_1(4))$ and consequently $\theta(\tau,s)\in M_{s/2}(\Gamma_1(4))$ for all even $s\ge2$.
>
> (b) Use Chapter3 to show $S_{s/2}(\Gamma_1(4))=0$ for $s=2,6,8$. Thus $\theta(\tau,s)$ is a linear combination of Eisenstein series. Compute $\dim M_{s/2}(\Gamma_1(4))$ in each case. (Hints are at the end of the book.)
>
> (c) For the number $r(n,s)$ of ordered signed representations of positive $n$ as $s$ squares, use Theorem4.8.1 to show $r(n,2)=4\sum_{0<m\mid n,\ m\text{ odd}}(-1)^{(m-1)/2}$. Similarly use Theorem4.5.2 to find $r(n,6)$ and $r(n,8)$.
>
> (d) The later proof of Proposition5.9.1 gives $|a_n(f)|\le Cn^{k/2}$ for every cusp form $f\in S_k(\Gamma)$. For even $s\ge10$, argue that Eisenstein coefficients in $M_{s/2}(\Gamma_1(4))$ dominate cusp coefficients, giving an asymptotic solution by ignoring cusp forms.

## Hints

> [!hint]- Hint 1
> The two-variable theta has character $\chi_{-4}$ at level four. Its powers have the required weights.

> [!hint]- Hint 2
> Compare the first coefficients in the Eisenstein bases; dimensions are one, two and three.

> [!hint]- Hint 3
> For a relative asymptotic, a growth upper bound alone is insufficient: the actual Eisenstein part needs a positive lower bound.

## Solution

> [!success]- Solution
> **Proof scope:** Independent derivation.
>
> Write $\Theta(\tau)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}q^{n^2}$, $F=\Theta^2$, so $\theta(\tau,s)=\Theta(\tau)^s$. Gaussian Poisson summation gives $\Theta(-1/(4\tau))=(-2i\tau)^{1/2}\Theta(\tau)$, with the analytic square root positive on the imaginary axis. Consequently, for $W\tau=-1/(4\tau)$, $F(W\tau)=-2i\tau F(\tau)$. Also $F(\tau+1)=F(\tau)$ directly from the series. Put $U=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\4&1\end{pmatrix}$; since $U=W T^{-1}W$ as fractional linear maps, applying these two identities gives
> $$
> F(U\tau)=(-2i)(W\tau-1)(-2i\tau)F(\tau)=(4\tau+1)F(\tau).
> $$
> The group $\Gamma_1(4)$ is generated by $T,U$. One precise input for this elementary presentation is $\Gamma(2)=\langle-I,T^2,ST^2S^{-1}\rangle$; conjugation by the matrix of Exercise3.9.4 sends the last two generators to $U^{-1},T$, giving $\Gamma_0(4)=\langle-I,T,U\rangle$, and its subgroup with diagonal residues one is $\langle T,U\rangle$. Thus the displayed transformations prove the weight-one law on $\Gamma_1(4)$. The series is holomorphic on $\mathcal H$, and its Fourier coefficients count lattice points in a disk and are $O(n+1)$. Proposition1.2.4's polynomial-growth criterion for holomorphic weakly modular functions therefore gives holomorphy at every cusp. This proves(a); taking products gives every even $s$.
>
> **(b)** The curve for $\Gamma_1(4)$ has genus zero, two regular cusps and one irregular cusp. The weight-one criterion gives $\dim M_1=1$, $S_1=0$. The odd formula at weight three gives $\dim M_3=2$, $\dim S_3=0$. The even formula at weight four gives $\dim M_4=3$, $\dim S_4=0$. These are the three requested cases.
>
> **(c)** Put $\chi=\chi_{-4}$, equal to zero on even integers and $(-1)^{(d-1)/2}$ on odd integers. The normalized weight-one series has constant $L(0,\chi)=1/2$ and coefficients $2\sum_{d\mid n}\chi(d)$. Thus the one-dimensional comparison of constants gives $\Theta^2=2E_1^{1,\chi}$, yielding the stated two-square formula.
>
> At weight three, set $A(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\chi(n/d)d^2$, $B(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\chi(d)d^2$. Theorem4.5.2 gives a basis consisting of the series with coefficients $2A(n)$ and $2B(n)$; their constants are respectively zero and $L(-2,\chi)=-1/2$. (The latter follows from the generalized Bernoulli evaluation.) Comparing constant and $q$ coefficients gives
> $$
> \Theta^6=8E_3^{\chi,1}-2E_3^{1,\chi},\qquad
> \boxed{r(n,6)=16A(n)-4B(n).}
> $$
> This comparison is sufficient: in this two-dimensional space the two basis vectors have independent constant and first coefficients. At weight four the three independent forms $E_4(\tau),E_4(2\tau),E_4(4\tau)$ form a basis (their first possible nonconstant exponents are $1,2,4$). Comparing constant, $q$, and $q^2$ gives
> $$
> \Theta^8=\frac{E_4(\tau)-2E_4(2\tau)+16E_4(4\tau)}{15}.
> $$
> These three comparisons determine a vector in this basis: the first fixes the first coefficient, the second fixes the second, and the constant fixes the third. Since $E_4=1+240\sum\sigma_3(n)q^n$,
> $$
> \boxed{r(n,8)=16\bigl(\sigma_3(n)-2\sigma_3(n/2)+16\sigma_3(n/4)\bigr)=16\sum_{d\mid n}(-1)^{n+d}d^3,}
> $$
> with nonintegral-argument divisor sums interpreted as zero. The last identity follows by separating powers of two in $n$ and in its divisors. At $n=1$ these formulas give $4,12,16$, agreeing with choosing one coordinate $\pm1$.
>
> **(d)** Put $k=s/2\ge5$ and write $\Theta^s=E+C$ with $C$ cuspidal. The quoted estimate gives $a_n(C)=O(n^{k/2})$. To make the relative conclusion rigorous, use the following additional standard input: the Eisenstein coefficient formula for a positive definite integral sum of $s\ge5$ squares identifies
> $$
> a_n(E)=\frac{\pi^k}{\Gamma(k)}n^{k-1}\mathfrak S_s(n),\qquad
> \mathfrak S_s(n)=\sum_{q\ge1}q^{-s}\sum_{\substack{a\ (q)\\\gcd(a,q)=1}}e^{-2\pi ian/q}\left(\sum_{x\ (q)}e^{2\pi iax^2/q}\right)^s.
> $$
> This is the classical Eisenstein/singular-series formula; its identification is an imported input, not a consequence merely of coefficient upper bounds. Its needed positivity can be checked quantitatively here. For odd $q$ the primitive quadratic Gauss sum has absolute value $\sqrt q$; for $q\equiv2\pmod4$ it vanishes; and for $4\mid q$ it has absolute value $\sqrt{2q}$. Consequently the absolute sum of all terms after $q=1$ is at most
> $$
> \sum_{q\ge3}q^{1-k}+2^k\sum_{4\mid q}q^{1-k}
> \le\zeta(k-1)-1-2^{1-k}+2^{2-k}\zeta(k-1)<1\quad(k\ge5).
> $$
> For the last inequality it suffices to use $\zeta(4)<1.083$, giving a bound below $0.156$ at $k=5$; all summands decrease with $k$. Thus $\mathfrak S_s(n)$ is bounded below by a positive constant uniformly in $n$. It follows that
> $$
> \frac{a_n(C)}{a_n(E)}=O(n^{1-k/2})\longrightarrow0,\qquad \frac{a_n(E)}{r(n,s)}\longrightarrow1.
> $$
> Computing $E$ from the finite Eisenstein basis therefore gives the claimed asymptotic. The lower bound explains the noncancellation condition hidden in the informal word “dominate.”
