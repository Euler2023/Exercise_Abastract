# Localization and Laurent Polynomials

## Definition

> [!info] Definition
> If $S\subseteq R$ is multiplicatively closed, the localization $S^{-1}R$ is the ring of fractions $a/s$ with $a\in R$, $s\in S$, modulo the localization equivalence relation. It is universal among homomorphisms from $R$ that send every element of $S$ to a unit.

## Intuition

Localization permits selected denominators while changing the rest of the ring as little as possible. If a selected denominator annihilates an element, that element must disappear after the denominator becomes invertible.

## Key Properties

- Adjoining an inverse of one element $a$ gives $R[x]/(ax-1)$.
- The kernel of $R\to R[a^{-1}]$ consists of elements killed by a power of $a$.
- Inverting $t$ in $F[t]$ gives the Laurent polynomial ring $F[t,t^{-1}]$.
- Inverting $t$ in $F[[t]]$ gives the Laurent series field $F((t))$.
- If $R$ is a domain and $S=R\setminus\{0\}$, then $S^{-1}R$ is the fraction field.

## Fractions over Rings with Zero Divisors

Let $A$ be a commutative ring with identity and let $S$ be multiplicatively closed with $1\in S$. The precise fraction relation is

$$
\frac as=\frac{a'}{s'}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
t(s'a-sa')=0\text{ for some }t\in S.
$$

The extra annihilating factor cannot be cancelled in a ring with zero divisors. The operations are

$$
\frac as+\frac bt=\frac{at+bs}{st},
\qquad
\frac as\frac bt=\frac{ab}{st}.
$$

In particular, $a/s=0$ exactly when some element of $S$ annihilates $a$. The equivalence relation and well-defined operations are proved in [[04 - Linear Algebra and Modules/Exercises/Exercise LA543 - Localization of Modules over Rings with Zero Divisors|Neukirch I.11.1]].

### Prime Ideals and Quotients

Extension and contraction give a bijection

$$
\{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}A:\mathfrak p\cap S=\varnothing\}
\longleftrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}(A_S),
\qquad \mathfrak p\longmapsto\mathfrak p A_S.
$$

For such a prime, if $\bar S$ is the image of $S$ in $A/\mathfrak p$, then

$$
A_S/\mathfrak p A_S\cong(A/\mathfrak p)_{\bar S}.
$$

Indeed, $a/s\mapsto\bar a/\bar s$ is onto and has kernel precisely the fractions with numerator in $\mathfrak p$, because $A/\mathfrak p$ is a domain and $\bar S$ contains no zero. Conversely, a prime of $A_S$ contains $a/s$ exactly when it contains $a/1$, since $s/1$ is a unit. These observations prove the bijection and the quotient formula; see [[02 - Ring Theory/Exercises/Exercise R362 - Prime Ideals and Quotients under Localization|Neukirch I.11.2]] for the full derivation.

### Universal Property and Successive Localization

For a unital ring map $f:A\to B$ with $f(S)\subseteq B^\times$, the unique extension is

$$
\widetilde f:A_S\to B,\qquad a/s\longmapsto f(a)f(s)^{-1}.
$$

Applying $f$ to an annihilating witness and cancelling its unit image proves representative independence, even if $A$ or $B$ has zero divisors. This universal property gives

$$
A_{ST}\cong(A_S)_{T^*},
$$

where $1\in S,T$, $ST=\{st:s\in S,t\in T\}$ and $T^*$ is the image of $T$ in $A_S$. Both rings universally invert the elements of both sets. The maps and their inverse identities are proved in [[02 - Ring Theory/Exercises/Exercise R363 - Iterated Localization and Products of Multiplicative Sets|Neukirch I.11.4]] and [[02 - Ring Theory/Exercises/Exercise R364 - The Universal Mapping Property of Localization|Neukirch I.11.5]].

> [!warning] Zero-ring convention
> It is possible that $0\notin S,T$ but $0\in ST$. To state the successive-localization formula without further hypotheses, allow localization at a set containing zero; all its fractions are then equal, giving the zero ring. For example, in $k\times k$, the sets generated by $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ individually avoid zero but their product contains zero. If localizations at zero are excluded, add $0\notin ST$ to the formula's hypotheses.

For a domain $A$ with $0\notin S$, an integral localization is trivial: if $A_S$ is integral over $A$, every $1/s$ satisfies a monic equation; multiplying that equation by one fewer power of $s$ shows $1/s\in A$. Hence $A_S=A$. No integrally closed hypothesis is needed; see [[02 - Ring Theory/Exercises/Exercise R365 - An Integral Localization of a Domain Is Trivial|Neukirch I.11.6]].

## Examples

> [!example] Product ring
> Localizing $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$ at $(2,0)$ kills the second factor because $(0,1)(2,0)=0$, leaving $\mathbb R$.
