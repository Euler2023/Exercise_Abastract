# Clifford Algebras

## Definition

> [!info] Lang's quadratic-relation convention
> Let $E$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $k$ and let $g:E\times E\to k$ be symmetric bilinear. The Clifford algebra is
>
> $$
> C_g(E)=T(E)/\langle v\otimes v-g(v,v)1:v\in E\rangle.
> $$
>
> Thus $v^2=g(v,v)1$ and
>
> $$
> uv+vu=2g(u,v)1.
> $$
>
> A linear map $c:E\to A$ into a unital $k$-algebra with central scalar action extends uniquely to an algebra map $C_g(E)\to A$ exactly when $c(v)^2=g(v,v)1$ for every $v$.

The universal property follows by extending to the tensor algebra and then factoring through the defining ideal. The sign is part of the convention: some sources use $v^2=-g(v,v)$ instead. Over characteristic $2$, the square relation remains the definition; the anticommutator equation alone does not recover it. No nondegeneracy assumption is needed for the definition or basis theorem.

This algebra is different from **Clifford theory** for restriction of finite-group representations to normal subgroups.

## Intuition

The exterior algebra imposes $v^2=0$. The Clifford algebra replaces this by a scalar measuring the quadratic form. Reordering two factors changes the sign and adds a lower-degree scalar term. The exterior algebra therefore records the highest-degree part of Clifford multiplication.

## Key Properties

### Ordered basis and filtration

For any basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ of $E$, the products

$$
e_I=e_{i_1}\cdots e_{i_r},
\qquad i_1<\cdots<i_r,
$$

including the empty product $1$, form a basis. In particular $\dim_k C_g(E)=2^n$, and $E$ embeds in $C_g(E)$.

The relations reorder every word into a sum of increasing monomials. Their independence can be seen on $\bigwedge E$: let $\epsilon_v$ be exterior multiplication and let $\iota_{g(v,-)}$ be contraction with $g(v,-)$. Then

$$
c(v)=\epsilon_v+\iota_{g(v,-)}
$$

has square $g(v,v)I$. Applying an increasing product of these operators to $1$ has highest component $e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_r}$. Comparing the largest degree in a putative linear relation proves independence. The complete contraction and descending-degree proof appears in the linked Exercise XIX.16; it works in every characteristic.

Set $F_{-1}=0$ and $F_i=(k+E)^i$. Its basis consists of the $e_I$ with $\lvert I\rvert\le i$, and the natural symbol map gives

$$
\bigwedge E\cong\operatorname{gr}C_g(E),\qquad
v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_i\longmapsto
v_1\cdots v_i+F_{i-1}.
$$

This isomorphism is functorial for linear maps preserving the forms. It is an isomorphism of associated graded algebras; it does not identify the original algebra multiplication with exterior multiplication.

### Parity and supercommutators

The defining relations have even parity, so

$$
C_g(E)=C^+\oplus C^-
$$

is graded by $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$, with the basis monomials partitioned according to their lengths. For homogeneous $a,b$, put

$$
\{a,b\}=ab-(-1)^{\deg(a)\deg(b)}ba.
$$

If $g$ is nondegenerate over $\mathbb R$ and $n=\dim E>0$, the real linear span of these supercommutators is $F_{n-1}$. In an orthogonal normalized basis, a proper monomial $e_I$ and an index $j\notin I$ satisfy

$$
\{e_j,e_je_I\}=2e_j^2e_I.
$$

Conversely, only complementary disjoint index sets could yield a top-degree product, and their supercommutator cancels. This proves the span assertion; it is not an assertion about an ideal generated by the brackets.

### An exterior representation over the complex numbers

Suppose a nondegenerate complex quadratic space splits as $E_{\mathbb C}=U\oplus V$ into two isotropic subspaces which pair perfectly. On $S=\bigwedge U$ define

$$
c(u+v)=\epsilon_u+2\iota_{g(v,-)}.
$$

Its square is $2g(u,v)I=g(u+v,u+v)I$, so it gives a Clifford representation. With dual bases, creation and deletion operators generate the vacuum projection and every matrix unit on $S$, proving

$$
C_g(E)_{\mathbb C}\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb C}(S)
$$

when $E_{\mathbb C}$ is the complexification of the underlying real quadratic space. An orthogonal complex structure $J$ with $J^2=-I$ supplies such a splitting by its $i,-i$ eigenspaces. The complete proof, including matrix units, is in the linked Exercise XIX.18.

### Real signs and periodicity

Lang writes $C_n$ for the negative definite form on $\mathbb R^n$ and $C'_n$ for the positive definite form. Thus negative generators square to $-1$, and positive generators square to $1$. The prime is unrelated to parity.

$$
C_1\cong\mathbb C,\qquad C_2\cong\mathbb H,\qquad
C'_1\cong\mathbb R\times\mathbb R,\qquad C'_2\cong M_2(\mathbb R).
$$

With ordinary real tensor products,

$$
C_{n+2}\cong C'_n\otimes C_2,\qquad
C'_{n+2}\cong C_n\otimes C'_2,\qquad
C_{n+8}\cong C_n\otimes M_{16}(\mathbb R).
$$

The explicit generator maps and the eight initial algebras are derived in the linked Exercise XIX.21. They show that every $C_n$ is a finite product of matrix algebras over $\mathbb R,\mathbb C,\mathbb H$, hence semisimple.

## Examples

> [!example] Zero form
> If $g=0$, the defining quotient is exactly $\bigwedge E$. In particular, when $E\ne0$, its positive-degree ideal is nonzero and nilpotent. Thus semisimplicity cannot be asserted for arbitrary degenerate Clifford algebras.

> [!example] A negative and a positive line
> In a negative line the generator behaves as $i$ in $\mathbb C$. In a positive line the idempotents $(1+e)/2,(1-e)/2$ yield the two factors of $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$.
