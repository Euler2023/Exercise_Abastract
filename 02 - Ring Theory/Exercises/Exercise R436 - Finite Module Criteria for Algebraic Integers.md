# Exercise R436: Finite Module Criteria for Algebraic Integers

## Problem Statement

> [!question] Original Exercise 6.4.5
> Prove Theorem 6.4.5 and its corollaries.

> **Referenced context.** Theorem 6.4.5 says that for $\alpha\in\mathbb C$ the following are equivalent: (1) $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer; (2) $\mathbb Z[\alpha]$ is finitely generated as an abelian group; (3) $\alpha$ belongs to a ring $R\subset\mathbb C$ finitely generated as an abelian group. Corollary 6.4.6 says the algebraic integers $\overline{\mathbb Z}$ form a ring. Corollary 6.4.7 says this ring is integrally closed, and states that every monic polynomial with coefficients in $\overline{\mathbb Z}$ splits into linear factors there.

## Hints

> [!hint]- Hint 1
> A monic equation reduces all powers to finitely many. In the opposite direction, let multiplication by $\alpha$ act on finite additive generators and apply the adjugate determinant identity.

> [!hint]- Hint 2
> If several numbers are integral, the ring they generate has a finite product spanning set over $\mathbb Z$. Apply this once to sums and products, and again to a root of a monic equation over that ring.

## Solution

> [!success]- Solution
> **Proof scope:** Independent derivation.
>
> **The finite-module criterion.** If $\alpha$ satisfies a monic polynomial of degree $n$ over $\mathbb Z$, its equation expresses $\alpha^n$ as an integer combination of $1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{n-1}$. Multiplying repeatedly by $\alpha$ proves every higher power is in the same additive span. Hence $\mathbb Z[\alpha]$ is finitely generated as an abelian group. This proves (1)$\Rightarrow$(2), and (2)$\Rightarrow$(3) follows by taking $R=\mathbb Z[\alpha]$.
>
> For (3)$\Rightarrow$(1), let $r_1,\ldots,r_m$ be additive generators of a unital subring $R\subseteq\mathbb C$ containing $\alpha$. There are integers $a_{ij}$ with $\alpha r_i=\sum_j a_{ij}r_j$. Set $A=(a_{ij})$ and $r=(r_i)^t$. Then
> $$
> (\alpha I-A)r=0.
> $$
> Multiplying by the adjugate matrix gives $\det(\alpha I-A)r_i=0$ for every $i$. At least one generator is nonzero because $1\in R$. Since $\mathbb C$ is a field, $\det(\alpha I-A)=0$. The polynomial $\det(xI-A)$ is monic with integer coefficients, proving integrality. The generators need not be an additive basis for this determinant trick.
>
> **Corollary 6.4.6: closure under ring operations.** If $\alpha,\beta$ satisfy monic integer equations of degrees $m,n$, the ring $\mathbb Z[\alpha,\beta]$ is additively generated by
> $$
> \{\alpha^i\beta^j:0\le i<m,\ 0\le j<n\}.
> $$
> Reduction by the two monic equations proves the spanning assertion. Both $\alpha+\beta$ and $\alpha\beta$ lie in this finite additive ring, so the criterion makes them integral. Also $-\alpha$ lies in the finite ring $\mathbb Z[\alpha]$, while $0,1$ are integral. Thus all complex algebraic integers form a ring.
>
> **Corollary 6.4.7: integrality is transitive here.** Suppose $\gamma\in\mathbb C$ is a root of
> $$
> x^n+c_1x^{n-1}+\cdots+c_n,\qquad c_i\in\overline{\mathbb Z}.
> $$
> By repeated use of the product spanning argument, $R=\mathbb Z[c_1,\ldots,c_n]$ has finite additive generators $r_1,\ldots,r_t$. The monic equation makes $R[\gamma]$ additively generated by the finitely many elements
> $$
> \{r_i\gamma^j:1\le i\le t,\ 0\le j<n\}.
> $$
> The criterion therefore makes $\gamma$ an algebraic integer. Every complex root is consequently in $\overline{\mathbb Z}$; using the fundamental theorem of algebra, the monic polynomial splits into linear factors over this ring. In particular any element of its fraction field integral over it belongs to it, which proves ordinary integral closedness as well.
