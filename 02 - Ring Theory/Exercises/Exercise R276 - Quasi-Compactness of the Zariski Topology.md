# Exercise R276: Quasi-Compactness of the Zariski Topology

## Problem Statement

> [!question]
> Prove that the Zariski topology is **compact**, in other words: let $\{U_i\}_{i \in I}$ be a family of open sets such that
> $$
> \bigcup_i U_i = \operatorname{spec}(A).
> $$
> Show that there is a finite number of open sets $U_{i_1}, \ldots, U_{i_n}$ whose union is $\operatorname{spec}(A)$.
> 
> *[Hint: Use closed sets, and use the fact that if a sum of ideals is the unit ideal, then 1 can be written as a finite sum of elements.]*

## Hints

> [!hint]- Hint 1
> A topological space is compact (in modern terminology, quasi-compact, since the Zariski topology is generally not Hausdorff) if every open cover has a finite subcover.
> 
> Pass to the closed complements: if $U_i = \operatorname{spec}(A) \setminus V(\mathfrak{a}_i)$, what is $\bigcap_{i \in I} V(\mathfrak{a}_i)$?

> [!hint]- Hint 2
> Recall the fundamental identity for families of ideals in the Zariski topology:
> $$
> \bigcap_{i \in I} V(\mathfrak{a}_i) = V\left( \sum_{i \in I} \mathfrak{a}_i \right).
> $$
> Under what condition on an ideal $\mathfrak{J}$ is $V(\mathfrak{J}) = \varnothing$?
> How is the sum of an arbitrary family of ideals defined?

## Solution

> [!success]- Solution
> Let $A$ be a commutative ring with identity element $1$, and let $X = \operatorname{spec}(A)$ be equipped with the Zariski topology.
> Recall that a subset $Y \subseteq X$ is closed if and only if $Y = V(\mathfrak{a})$ for some ideal $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq A$, where
> $$
> V(\mathfrak{a}) = \{\mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{spec}(A) : \mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{p}\}.
> $$
> An open subset $U \subseteq X$ is therefore the complement of a closed subset:
> $$
> U = X \setminus V(\mathfrak{a}) = D(\mathfrak{a}) = \{\mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{spec}(A) : \mathfrak{a} \not\subseteq \mathfrak{p}\}.
> $$
> 
> Let $\{U_i\}_{i \in I}$ be an arbitrary family of open sets covering $X$:
> $$
> \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i = X = \operatorname{spec}(A).
> $$
> For each $i \in I$, write $U_i = X \setminus V(\mathfrak{a}_i)$ for some ideal $\mathfrak{a}_i \subseteq A$.
> 
> ### Step 1: Reformulation in Terms of Closed Sets
> 
> Taking complements in $X$ of the open covering identity:
> $$
> X \setminus \left( \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i \right) = X \setminus X = \varnothing.
> $$
> By De Morgan's laws:
> $$
> \bigcap_{i \in I} (X \setminus U_i) = \bigcap_{i \in I} V(\mathfrak{a}_i) = \varnothing.
> $$
> 
> ### Step 2: Intersection of Closed Sets and Ideal Sums
> 
> A prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ belongs to the intersection $\bigcap_{i \in I} V(\mathfrak{a}_i)$ if and only if $\mathfrak{a}_i \subseteq \mathfrak{p}$ for every $i \in I$.
> This holds if and only if the ideal generated by all the $\mathfrak{a}_i$ is contained in $\mathfrak{p}$:
> $$
> \sum_{i \in I} \mathfrak{a}_i \subseteq \mathfrak{p}.
> $$
> Therefore, we have the exact set identity:
> $$
> \bigcap_{i \in I} V(\mathfrak{a}_i) = V\left( \sum_{i \in I} \mathfrak{a}_i \right).
> $$
> 
> Since the intersection is empty, we have:
> $$
> V\left( \sum_{i \in I} \mathfrak{a}_i \right) = \varnothing.
> $$
> 
> ### Step 3: Finiteness from the Unit Ideal
> 
> For any ideal $\mathfrak{J} \subseteq A$, $V(\mathfrak{J}) = \varnothing$ if and only if $\mathfrak{J}$ is not contained in any prime ideal of $A$.
> By Krull's Theorem (a standard consequence of Zorn's lemma), every proper ideal of a commutative ring with identity is contained in at least one maximal ideal (hence a prime ideal).
> Therefore, $V(\mathfrak{J}) = \varnothing$ forces $\mathfrak{J}$ to be the entire ring:
> $$
> \sum_{i \in I} \mathfrak{a}_i = A = (1).
> $$
> 
> In particular, the identity element $1 \in A$ belongs to the sum ideal $\sum_{i \in I} \mathfrak{a}_i$.
> By the definition of the sum of an arbitrary family of ideals, an element of $\sum_{i \in I} \mathfrak{a}_i$ is a finite sum of elements from the individual ideals.
> Therefore, there exists a finite subset of indices $\{i_1, i_2, \ldots, i_n\} \subseteq I$ and elements $x_{i_j} \in \mathfrak{a}_{i_j}$ such that
> $$
> 1 = x_{i_1} + x_{i_2} + \cdots + x_{i_n}.
> $$
> 
> ### Step 4: Extracting the Finite Subcover
> 
> Since $x_{i_j} \in \mathfrak{a}_{i_j}$ for each $j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$, the sum of these $n$ ideals contains $1$:
> $$
> 1 \in \mathfrak{a}_{i_1} + \mathfrak{a}_{i_2} + \cdots + \mathfrak{a}_{i_n} \implies \sum_{j=1}^n \mathfrak{a}_{i_j} = A.
> $$
> Consequently,
> $$
> \bigcap_{j=1}^n V(\mathfrak{a}_{i_j}) = V\left( \sum_{j=1}^n \mathfrak{a}_{i_j} \right) = V(A) = \varnothing.
> $$
> Taking complements in $X$ once again:
> $$
> \bigcup_{j=1}^n U_{i_j} = \bigcup_{j=1}^n (X \setminus V(\mathfrak{a}_{i_j})) = X \setminus \left( \bigcap_{j=1}^n V(\mathfrak{a}_{i_j}) \right) = X \setminus \varnothing = X = \operatorname{spec}(A).
> $$
> 
> Thus, the finite subcollection $\{U_{i_1}, \ldots, U_{i_n}\}$ covers $\operatorname{spec}(A)$.
> This proves that the topological space $\operatorname{spec}(A)$ is compact (quasi-compact).

## Notes

- **Independent derivation:** The proof follows Lang's hint directly, using the duality between ideal sums and intersections of closed sets, together with the unit representation $1 = \sum_{j=1}^n x_{i_j}$.
- **Terminology (Compact vs. Quasi-compact):** In Bourbaki and modern algebraic geometry (e.g. Hartshorne, EGA), "compact" is reserved for Hausdorff spaces, and non-Hausdorff spaces satisfying the Heine-Borel finite subcover property are called **quasi-compact**. Serge Lang uses the classical terminology "compact" without assuming the Hausdorff separation axiom.
