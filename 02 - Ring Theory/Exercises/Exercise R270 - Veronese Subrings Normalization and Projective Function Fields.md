# Exercise R270: Veronese Subrings Normalization and Projective Function Fields

## Problem Statement

> [!question]
> **Shared setup for Exercises 5–9.** Let $R=k[x]=k[X]/\mathfrak{p}$, where $\mathfrak{p}$ is a homogeneous prime ideal. Thus $(x)$ is a homogeneous generic point of a $k$-variety. Let $K=k(x)=\operatorname{Frac}(R)$ and let $I$ be the integral closure of $R$ in $K$. Assume, as in the source, that $K/k$ is a regular extension.
>
> Write $R_d$ and $I_d$ for their homogeneous degree-$d$ pieces, with the decomposition of $I$ supplied by Exercise 6. Fix an integer $m\ge1$. As in Exercise 8, $m$ is **well behaved** when $I_m^q=I_{qm}$ for every $q\ge1$, where a product of subspaces means the $k$-linear span of products.
>
> **(a)** Prove that $I_m$ is a finite dimensional vector space over $k$. Let $w_0, \ldots, w_M$ be a basis for $I_m$ over $k$. Then $k[I_m] = k[w]$.
> 
> **(b)** If $m$ is well behaved, show that $k[I_m]$ is integrally closed.
> 
> **(c)** Denote by $k((x))$ the field generated over $k$ by all quotients $x_i/x_j$ with $x_j \neq 0$, and similarly for $k((w))$. Show that $k((x)) = k((w))$.
> 
> *(If you want to see Exercises 4–9 worked out, see my Introduction to Algebraic Geometry, Interscience 1958, Chapter V.)*

## Hints

> [!hint]- Hint 1
> For part (a), recall from Exercise 7 that $I_m = \sum_{i=1}^s R_{m - d_i} z_i$.
> What is the dimension of the space of homogeneous polynomials of a given degree in $n$ variables over $k$?
> 
> For part (b), first use the regular-extension hypothesis to prove $I_0=k$. If $m$ is well behaved, $I_m^q=I_{qm}$ then gives $k[I_m]=\bigoplus_{q\ge0}I_{qm}=I^{(m)}$, the $m$-th Veronese subring.
> If $u \in \operatorname{Frac}(I^{(m)})$ is integral over $I^{(m)}$, show first that $u \in I$, and then use the grading of $u$ to show that $u \in I^{(m)}$.

> [!hint]- Hint 2
> For part (c), both $k((x))$ and $k((w))$ are subfields of $k(x)$ consisting of homogeneous fractions of degree $0$.
> 
> Since each $w_\nu \in I_m \subset k(x)$ is homogeneous of degree $m$, what is the degree of $w_\mu / w_\nu$?
> 
> For the reverse inclusion, each $x_i x_j^{m-1}$ is in $R_m \subseteq I_m = \operatorname{span}_k(w)$. What happens when you divide by $x_j^m \in R_m$?

## Solution

> [!success]- Solution
> Keep the shared notation $R=k[x]=k[X]/\mathfrak{p}$, $K=\operatorname{Frac}(R)$, and $I$, with $K/k$ regular. By Exercise 6, $I=\bigoplus_{d\ge0}I_d$.
>
> ### Part (a): Finite-dimensional homogeneous pieces
>
> By Exercise 7, there are finitely many homogeneous elements $z_1,\ldots,z_s$ of degrees $d_i\ge0$ such that $I=\sum_iRz_i$. Taking degree $m$ gives
> $$
> I_m=\sum_{i=1}^sR_{m-d_i}z_i,
> $$
> with $R_e=0$ for $e<0$. For $e\ge0$, $R_e$ is a quotient of the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree $e$ in $n$ variables, so
> $$
> \dim_kR_e\le\binom{n+e-1}{e}<\infty.
> $$
> Every summand $R_{m-d_i}z_i$ is an image of a finite-dimensional space. Hence their finite sum $I_m$ is finite-dimensional. A basis $w_0,\ldots,w_M$ spans $I_m$, so the two sets generate the same $k$-algebra:
> $$
> k[I_m]=k[w_0,\ldots,w_M]=k[w].
> $$
> If $R_1=0$, then $R=K=I=k$, so $I_m=0$ for $m\ge1$. With an empty basis and the convention that a field or algebra generated by the empty set over $k$ is $k$, all three parts hold. We may therefore assume $R_1\ne0$; a nonzero $x_j$ then gives $x_j^m\in I_m\setminus\{0\}$.
>
> ### Part (b): Integral closedness of the Veronese subring
>
> First, we verify the degree-zero term. If $a\in I_0$, choose an integral equation over $R$ and take its degree-zero component:
> $$
> a^N+c_{N-1,0}a^{N-1}+\cdots+c_{0,0}=0,
> \qquad c_{j,0}\in R_0=k.
> $$
> Thus $a$ is algebraic over $k$. A regular extension has $k$ algebraically closed in $K$, so $a\in k$. Consequently $I_0=k$.
>
> Assume that $m$ is well behaved. Since multiplication adds degrees and $I_m^q=I_{qm}$ for every $q\ge1$,
> $$
> A:=k[I_m]=k\oplus\bigoplus_{q\ge1}I_m^q
> =\bigoplus_{q\ge0}I_{qm}=:I^{(m)}.
> $$
> Let $\alpha\in\operatorname{Frac}(A)$ be integral over $A$. Its monic equation is also an integral equation over $I$. Since the integral closure $I$ is integrally closed in $K$ by transitivity of integral dependence, we obtain $\alpha\in I$.
>
> Group the homogeneous pieces of $I$ according to their degree modulo $m$:
> $$
> I=\bigoplus_{r=0}^{m-1}M_r,\qquad
> M_r=\bigoplus_{\substack{d\ge0\\d\equiv r\;(\mathrm{mod}\;m)}}I_d,
> \qquad M_0=A.
> $$
> Choose a fraction representation $\alpha=u/v$ with $u,v\in A$ and $v\ne0$. Write $\alpha=\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\alpha^{[r]}$, where $\alpha^{[r]}\in M_r$. Multiplication by any element of $A$ preserves each $M_r$, even if that element is not homogeneous. Comparing the residue-degree components in
> $$
> u=v\alpha=\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}v\alpha^{[r]}
> $$
> gives $v\alpha^{[r]}=0$ for every $r\ne0$. The ring $I$ is a domain and $v\ne0$, so these components vanish. Therefore $\alpha\in M_0=A$, proving that $k[I_m]$ is integrally closed in its fraction field.
>
> ### Part (c): Equality of the degree-zero function fields
>
> We first justify that a homogeneous element $h\in K$ of degree zero belongs to $k((x))$. Write $h=P/Q$ with $P,Q\in R$ and $Q\ne0$. The identity $h(tx)=h(x)$ gives
> $$
> P(tx)=h(x)Q(tx).
> $$
> Write $P=\sum_dP_d$ and $Q=\sum_dQ_d$ in $R$ and compare coefficients of $t$. Choose a degree $d$ with $Q_d\ne0$; then $P_d=hQ_d$, so $h=P_d/Q_d$ is a quotient of homogeneous polynomials of the same degree. Fix a coordinate $x_j\ne0$. Dividing $P_d$ and $Q_d$ by $x_j^d$ expresses both as polynomials in the ratios $x_i/x_j$, whence $h\in k((x))$. Conversely, every coordinate ratio has degree zero, and the degree-zero elements are closed under field operations.
>
> Each $w_\mu$ has degree $m$, so every ratio $w_\mu/w_\nu$ with $w_\nu\ne0$ has degree zero. The preceding argument yields
> $$
> k((w))\subseteq k((x)).
> $$
> For the reverse inclusion, take $x_j\ne0$. The two degree-$m$ elements
> $$
> x_j^m,\qquad x_ix_j^{m-1}
> $$
> belong to $R_m\subseteq I_m$, so there are coefficients $c_\nu,d_\nu\in k$ such that
> $$
> x_j^m=\sum_{\nu=0}^M c_\nu w_\nu,\qquad
> x_ix_j^{m-1}=\sum_{\nu=0}^M d_\nu w_\nu.
> $$
> Divide both equations by any basis element $w_\lambda\ne0$. The right sides lie in $k((w))$, and $x_j^m/w_\lambda\ne0$. Their quotient is
> $$
> \frac{x_ix_j^{m-1}/w_\lambda}{x_j^m/w_\lambda}
> =\frac{x_i}{x_j}\in k((w)).
> $$
> These coordinate ratios generate $k((x))$, so $k((x))\subseteq k((w))$. Thus $k((x))=k((w))$. Part (c) uses only $m\ge1$ and does not require $m$ to be well behaved.

## Notes

- **Independent derivation and source scope:** The shared setup and all three parts of Exercise 9 are checked at [S2, Ch. IX, printed p. 410, PDF p. 425]. Parts (a) and (b) use the independently proved Exercises 6–8. The source also refers readers to Lang's *Introduction to Algebraic Geometry* (1958, Chapter V); that book was not consulted for this solution.
- **Regularity hypothesis:** Regularity ensures $I_0=k$. Omitting it can invalidate (b): for $k=\mathbb R$ and $R=\mathbb R[t,it]\subset\mathbb C[t]$, the integral closure is $I=\mathbb C[t]$ with $I_d=\mathbb C t^d$. Every $m\ge1$ is well behaved, but $\mathbb R[I_1]=\mathbb R+t\mathbb C[t]$ is not integrally closed, because $i$ lies in its fraction field and is integral over it without belonging to it.
- **Method boundary:** A general element of $\operatorname{Frac}(I^{(m)})$ need not admit a homogeneous denominator. For instance, $1/(1+x^2)\in k(x^2)$ cannot have a denominator $c x^{2q}$. Part (b) first uses integrality to put $\alpha$ in the graded ring $I$, then compares its degrees modulo $m$ using an arbitrary denominator in $I^{(m)}$.
