# Exercise R366: Normalizations of Four Affine Curve Rings

## Problem Statement

> [!question] Neukirch I.12.1
> Show that
> $$
> \mathbb C[X,Y]/(XY-X),\quad \mathbb C[X,Y]/(XY-1),
> $$
> $$
> \mathbb C[X,Y]/(X^2-Y^3),\quad \mathbb C[X,Y]/(Y^2-X^2-X^3)
> $$
> are one-dimensional noetherian rings. Which ones are integral domains? Determine their normalizations.
>
> **Printed hint:** For instance in the last example, put $t=X/Y$ and show that the homomorphism $\mathbb C[X,Y]\to\mathbb C[t]$, $X\mapsto t^2-1$, $Y\mapsto t(t^2-1)$, has kernel $(Y^2-X^2-X^3)$.

> [!warning] Source wording and scope
> The printed hint has the ratio $t=X/Y$ reversed. For its stated parametrization $X=t^2-1$, $Y=t(t^2-1)$, one has $t=Y/X$ in the fraction field. The original hint is retained above; its kernel assertion is valid. The first example is reduced but not a domain, so its normalization is taken in its total quotient ring.

## Hints

> [!hint]- Hint 1: Separate the reducible ring
> The first equation is $X(Y-1)=0$. Evaluate on its two components and describe the image in $\mathbb C[Y]\times\mathbb C[X]$.

> [!hint]- Hint 2: Parametrize the domains
> For the cusp take $X=t^3,Y=t^2$; for the node use the printed parametrization, with the corrected ratio $t=Y/X$. Divide by the monic quadratic to check each kernel.

> [!hint]- Hint 3: Adjoin the missing integral parameter
> The cusp has $t^2=Y$, and the node has $t^2=X+1$. The reducible ring acquires a separating idempotent in its total quotient ring.

## Solution

> [!success]- Independent derivation
>
> Write $A_1,A_2,A_3,A_4$ for the four rings, in printed order. Every one is Noetherian by Hilbert's basis theorem and the fact that a quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian. These two elementary commutative-algebra facts are the named external inputs here. For a reduced ring that is not a domain, normalization means integral closure in its **total quotient ring**, obtained by inverting all non-zero-divisors.
>
> ### 1. The two intersecting components
> Evaluation gives a map
> $$
> A_1\longrightarrow\mathbb C[Y]\times\mathbb C[X],\qquad
> h\longmapsto(h(0,Y),h(X,1)).
> $$
> The kernel before quotienting is $(X)\cap(Y-1)=(X(Y-1))$: divisibility by both distinct prime polynomials implies divisibility by their product. Its image is exactly
> $$
> A_1\cong\{(f,g):f(1)=g(0)\}.
> $$
> The compatibility is necessary by evaluation at $(0,1)$, and sufficient because $f(Y)+g(X)-f(1)$ lifts the pair. Thus $A_1$ is reduced. It is not a domain: the nonzero classes of $X$ and $Y-1$ have product zero.
>
> Every prime contains $X$ or $Y-1$. A chain beginning with such a prime is a chain in one component quotient, $\mathbb C[Y]$ or $\mathbb C[X]$, so has length at most one. The chain $(X)\subsetneq(X,Y)$ in $A_1$ has length one, proving dimension one.
>
> Its total quotient ring is $\mathbb C(Y)\times\mathbb C(X)$. Indeed a pair in $A_1$ is a non-zero-divisor exactly when both polynomial components are nonzero. To express any rational pair $(f_1/g_1,f_2/g_2)$, multiply its two numerator/denominator pairs respectively by $Y-1$ and $X$; both resulting pairs satisfy the compatibility value zero. In this quotient ring,
> $$
> e=(1,0)=\frac{(Y-1,0)}{(Y-1,X)}
> $$
> is integral, since $e^2-e=0$. Moreover $A_1[e]=\mathbb C[Y]\times\mathbb C[X]$: use $e(f,f(1))$ and $(1-e)(g(0),g)$ to obtain arbitrary components. This product is integrally closed, since integrality is componentwise and each polynomial ring is a UFD, hence integrally closed. It is integral over $A_1$ and has the same total quotient ring. An element integral over $A_1$ is also integral over this larger ring, so must already lie in it. Therefore
> $$
> \widetilde A_1=\mathbb C[Y]\times\mathbb C[X].
> $$
>
> ### 2. The hyperbola
> The maps $X\mapsto t$, $Y\mapsto t^{-1}$ give
> $$
> A_2\cong\mathbb C[t,t^{-1}].
> $$
> The inverse sends $t$ to the class of $X$, which is invertible with inverse the class of $Y$. This is a domain and a localization of the PID $\mathbb C[t]$, hence itself a PID and integrally closed. Its nonzero primes are generated by $t-c$ with $c\ne0$, so its dimension is one. It is its own normalization.
>
> ### 3. The cusp
> Under $X\mapsto t^3$, $Y\mapsto t^2$, divide a polynomial by the monic polynomial $X^2-Y^3$ in the variable $X$. Its remainder is $a(Y)+Xb(Y)$. After substitution it becomes $a(t^2)+t^3b(t^2)$, the sum of a polynomial with even exponents and one with odd exponents. It vanishes only if both polynomials vanish, hence $a=b=0$. Thus the kernel is exactly $(X^2-Y^3)$ and
> $$
> A_3\cong\mathbb C[t^2,t^3]
> $$
> is a domain. Its fraction field is $\mathbb C(t)$, because $t=X/Y$ in that field. The element $t$ is integral over $A_3$ by $t^2=Y$, so $\mathbb C[t]=A_3[t]$ is integral over $A_3$ and integrally closed. The same integral-closure argument as above proves $\widetilde A_3=\mathbb C[t]$.
>
> ### 4. The node
> Use $X\mapsto t^2-1$, $Y\mapsto t(t^2-1)$. Dividing by the monic polynomial $Y^2-X^2-X^3$ in $Y$ leaves $a(X)+Yb(X)$. Its image is
> $$
> a(t^2-1)+t(t^2-1)b(t^2-1).
> $$
> The two summands have even and odd parity in $t$. Vanishing forces both to be zero, and substitution $X\mapsto t^2-1$ is injective on $\mathbb C[X]$. Therefore $a=b=0$. The printed kernel is correct, and $A_4$ is the domain
> $$
> \mathbb C[t^2-1,t(t^2-1)].
> $$
> Its fraction field is $\mathbb C(t)$ with $t=Y/X$, and $t$ is integral because $t^2=X+1$. Hence $\widetilde A_4=\mathbb C[t]$.
>
> To finish the dimension check for $A_3,A_4$ without assuming a curve-dimension formula, each is a finite module over a polynomial subring $\mathbb C[u]$: take $u=Y$ for $A_3$ and $u=X$ for $A_4$, using its monic quadratic relation. Every element is integral over $\mathbb C[u]$ (apply Cayley–Hamilton to multiplication in the free module with basis $1,X$ or $1,Y$). If $0\ne a$ lies in a nonzero prime $P$, a monic integral equation for $a$ can be chosen with nonzero constant coefficient: cancel any initial power of $a$ from a relation, using the domain property. That coefficient is a nonzero element of $P\cap\mathbb C[u]$. Thus this contraction is a nonzero prime, hence maximal. The quotient $A_i/P$ is then a finite-dimensional domain over $\mathbb C$, hence a field (multiplication by a nonzero element is an injective, thus surjective, linear map). Every nonzero prime is maximal. The evaluation ideals at $(0,0)$ are nonzero maximal ideals in both domains, so their dimensions are exactly one.
>
> In summary, only $A_1$ is not a domain. The four normalizations, in order, are
> $$
> \mathbb C[Y]\times\mathbb C[X],\qquad
> \mathbb C[t,t^{-1}],\qquad\mathbb C[t],\qquad\mathbb C[t].
> $$
