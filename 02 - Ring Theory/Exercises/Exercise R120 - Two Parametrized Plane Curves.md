# Exercise R120: Two Parametrized Plane Curves

## Problem Statement

> [!question] Exercise 3.5
> **(a)** Consider the map $\psi:\mathbb C[x,y]\to\mathbb C[t]$ defined by
>
> $$
> f(x,y)\longmapsto f(t^2,t^3).
> $$
>
> Prove that its image is the set of polynomials $p(t)$ such that $p'(0)=0$.
>
> **(b)** Consider the map $\varphi:\mathbb C[x,y]\to\mathbb C[t]$ defined by
>
> $$
> f(x,y)\longmapsto f(t^2-t,t^3-t^2).
> $$
>
> Prove that $\ker\varphi$ is a principal ideal, and find a generator $g(x,y)$ for this ideal. Prove that the image of $\varphi$ is the set of polynomials $p(t)$ such that $p(0)=p(1)$. Give an intuitive explanation in terms of the geometry of the variety $\{g=0\}$ in $\mathbb C^2$.

## Hints

> [!hint]- Hint 1
> For part (a), the semigroup generated by $2$ and $3$ contains $0$ and every integer at least $2$, but not $1$.

> [!hint]- Hint 2
> In part (b), write $x=t(t-1)$ and $y=tx$. Reduce a polynomial modulo a monic quadratic in $y$, and use the involution $t\mapsto1-t$.

## Solution

> [!success]- Solution
> **(a)** The image is the subring
>
> $$
> \mathbb C[t^2,t^3].
> $$
>
> Every monomial in this subring has exponent $2a+3b$, so no term of degree $1$ occurs. Hence every image polynomial has zero coefficient of $t$, equivalently $p'(0)=0$.
>
> Conversely, if $p'(0)=0$, then $p$ has no $t$-term. Every even exponent is a multiple of $2$, and every odd exponent at least $3$ has the form $3+2a$. Thus every monomial of $p$ belongs to $\mathbb C[t^2,t^3]$, proving
>
> $$
> \operatorname{im}\psi
> =\{p\in\mathbb C[t]:p'(0)=0\}.
> $$
>
> **(b)** Set
>
> $$
> x=t^2-t=t(t-1),\qquad
> y=t^3-t^2=tx.
> $$
>
> These satisfy
>
> $$
> y^2-xy-x^3=0.
> $$
>
> Therefore
>
> $$
> g(x,y)=y^2-xy-x^3
> $$
>
> belongs to $\ker\varphi$.
>
> Since $g$ is monic of degree two in $y$, divide any $f\in\mathbb C[x,y]$ by $g$ as a polynomial in $y$:
>
> $$
> f=qg+a(x)y+b(x).
> $$
>
> Suppose $f\in\ker\varphi$. With $s=t^2-t$, the remainder gives
>
> $$
> a(s)ts+b(s)=0.
> $$
>
> Replacing $t$ by $1-t$ leaves $s$ fixed and gives
>
> $$
> a(s)(1-t)s+b(s)=0.
> $$
>
> Subtraction yields $a(s)(2t-1)s=0$. Since $\mathbb C[t]$ is a domain and $s(2t-1)\neq0$, we have $a(s)=0$. The substitution map $\mathbb C[x]\to\mathbb C[t]$, $x\mapsto t^2-t$, is injective because $t^2-t$ is transcendental over $\mathbb C$; hence $a=0$, and then $b=0$. Thus
>
> $$
> \ker\varphi=(y^2-xy-x^3).
> $$
>
> Every image polynomial takes the same value at $0$ and $1$, because both parameter values map to $(x,y)=(0,0)$. Conversely, if $p(0)=p(1)=c$, then $p(t)-c$ is divisible by $t(t-1)=s$, so
>
> $$
> p(t)=c+s\,q(t).
> $$
>
> The relation $t^2=t+s$ reduces every polynomial uniquely to
>
> $$
> q(t)=A(s)+tB(s)
> $$
>
> with $A,B\in\mathbb C[s]$. Consequently,
>
> $$
> p(t)=c+sA(s)+tsB(s),
> $$
>
> which is the image of $c+xA(x)+yB(x)$. Therefore
>
> $$
> \operatorname{im}\varphi
> =\{p\in\mathbb C[t]:p(0)=p(1)\}.
> $$
>
> Geometrically, the parametrization identifies the two parameter values $0$ and $1$ at the singular point $(0,0)$ of the curve $g=0$. Away from $x=0$, the parameter is recovered as $t=y/x$. Regular functions on the curve therefore correspond exactly to polynomials on the parameter line that cannot distinguish those two identified points.
