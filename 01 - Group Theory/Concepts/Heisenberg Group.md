# Heisenberg Group

## Definition

> [!info] Definition (Three-Dimensional Real Heisenberg Group)
> The **three-dimensional real Heisenberg group** is
> $$H=\left\{h(a,b,c)=\begin{bmatrix}1&a&b\\0&1&c\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}:a,b,c\in\mathbb R\right\}$$
>
> with matrix multiplication as its group operation.

Hall explicitly calls this matrix group the Heisenberg group. Artin Exercise 12.2 uses the same group without assigning it that name. [S10, Ch. 1, §1.2.6, printed pp. 11-12, PDF pp. 23-24; S1, Ch. 2, §12, Ex. 12.2, printed p. 75, PDF p. 87]

## Intuition

The three coordinates do not add independently. Multiplying two elements adds the $a$- and $c$-coordinates, but the middle coordinate acquires an extra cross term $ac'$. This cross term is the source of noncommutativity:

$$
h(a,b,c)h(a',b',c')
=h(a+a',b+b'+ac',c+c').
$$

All failures of commutativity occur in the $b$-coordinate. Consequently, the group becomes abelian after quotienting by that central direction.

## Group Law and Inverses

> [!abstract] Proposition
> For $a,b,c,a',b',c'\in\mathbb R$,
>
> $$
> h(a,b,c)h(a',b',c')
> =h(a+a',b+b'+ac',c+c'),
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> h(a,b,c)^{-1}=h(-a,ac-b,-c).
> $$
>
> Therefore $H$ is a subgroup of $GL_3(\mathbb R)$.
>
> **Proof.** Direct matrix multiplication gives the product formula, so $H$ is closed under multiplication. The identity is $h(0,0,0)$. Substituting $h(-a,ac-b,-c)$ into the product formula on either side gives $h(0,0,0)$, proving the inverse formula and the subgroup claim.

## Commutators, Center, and Quotient

Use the commutator convention $[x,y]=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$.

> [!abstract] Proposition
> Let
>
> $$
> K=\{h(0,b,0):b\in\mathbb R\}.
> $$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> Z(H)=[H,H]=K,
> \qquad
> H/K\cong(\mathbb R^2,+).
> $$
>
> **Proof.** Applying the product and inverse formulas gives
>
> $$
> [h(a,b,c),h(a',b',c')]
> =h(0,ac'-a'c,0).
> $$
>
> Hence every commutator lies in $K$. Conversely, every $h(0,t,0)$ is the commutator
>
> $$
> [h(t,0,0),h(0,0,1)]=h(0,t,0),
> $$
>
> so $[H,H]=K$. An element $h(a,b,c)$ is central exactly when $ac'-a'c=0$ for every $a',c'\in\mathbb R$. Taking $(a',c')=(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ forces $a=c=0$, and therefore $Z(H)=K$.
>
> Finally, the map
>
> $$
> \pi:H\longrightarrow(\mathbb R^2,+),
> \qquad
> h(a,b,c)\longmapsto(a,c),
> $$
>
> is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $K$. The First Isomorphism Theorem gives $H/K\cong(\mathbb R^2,+)$.

Equivalently, $H$ is a central extension

$$
1\longrightarrow(\mathbb R,+)
\xrightarrow{\,t\mapsto h(0,t,0)\,}
H\xrightarrow{\,\pi\,}
(\mathbb R^2,+)\longrightarrow1.
$$

## Nilpotence

> [!abstract] Proposition
> The Heisenberg group $H$ is nonabelian and nilpotent of class $2$.
>
> **Proof.** The commutator formula shows that $H$ is nonabelian, for example
>
> $$
> [h(1,0,0),h(0,0,1)]=h(0,1,0)\ne e.
> $$
>
> On the other hand, $[H,H]=K\subseteq Z(H)$, so
>
> $$
> [H,[H,H]]=[H,K]=\{e\}.
> $$
>
> Thus the lower central series terminates after two nontrivial steps. Moreover, the derived series is $H\supseteq[H,H]=K\supseteq[K,K]=\{e\}$, so $H$ is also solvable.

## Lie Algebra Perspective

The Lie algebra of $H$ is the vector space of strictly upper triangular real $3\times3$ matrices

$$
\mathfrak h=\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
0&a&b\\
0&0&c\\
0&0&0
\end{bmatrix}:a,b,c\in\mathbb R
\right\},
$$

with bracket $[X,Y]=XY-YX$. If

$$
X=E_{12},\qquad Y=E_{23},\qquad Z=E_{13},
$$

then

$$
[X,Y]=Z,\qquad [X,Z]=[Y,Z]=0.
$$

Hall proves the displayed description of $\mathfrak h$ as the Lie algebra of $H$. Kac's Heisenberg Lie algebra of order $n$ has basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n,f_1,\ldots,f_n,z$ with $[e_i,f_j]=\delta_{ij}z$ and all other basic brackets zero; the case $n=1$ has the same bracket pattern as $\mathfrak h$. [S10, Ch. 3, Prop. 3.26, printed p. 59, PDF p. 71; S9, Ch. 2, §2.9, printed p. 26, PDF p. 49]

## Key Properties

1. $H$ is a nonabelian subgroup of $GL_3(\mathbb R)$.
2. Its center and commutator subgroup coincide: $Z(H)=[H,H]=K$.
3. Its quotient by the center is the additive group $(\mathbb R^2,+)$.
4. It is nilpotent of class $2$ and therefore solvable.
5. Its Lie algebra has one-dimensional center generated by $E_{13}$.

## Examples

> [!example] The Cross Term Detects Noncommutativity
> The product formula gives
>
> $$
> h(1,0,2)h(3,4,5)=h(4,9,7),
> $$
>
> whereas
>
> $$
> h(3,4,5)h(1,0,2)=h(4,10,7).
> $$
>
> Their commutator is $h(0,-1,0)$, which lies in the center.

> [!example] Central Elements
> Every element $h(0,b,0)$ commutes with every element of $H$. Modulo these elements, only the coordinates $(a,c)$ remain, and their induced operation is ordinary vector addition.
